Reference

Restaurant website glossary.
Plain-English definitions, every term we reference.

Every concept the Muntin audit tool and the 30-point restaurant checklist lean on has a definition here. Every entry has a stable anchor, so the checklist's "Learn more" pop-overs deep-link straight into the one you need.

Basics

The five things a first-time visitor looks for before anything else: what you are, where you are, when you're open, and how to reach you.

Mobile-readable menu #

A menu page that renders as real HTML text on a phone — not a PDF, not a JPEG, not a Squarespace image gallery pretending to be a menu.

PDFs open in a separate tab, download to the visitor's camera roll, and do not zoom gracefully. Image menus can't be read by screen readers, can't be searched, and blur when pinch-zoomed. Real HTML menus are indexable by Google, legible on every screen size, and updatable without a designer. For tasting-menu formats where print aesthetics matter, ship BOTH — an HTML page for the site and a separate PDF for guests to download if they want.

Related: WCAG contrast Related: body font-size

Above-the-fold hours #

Today's open/close times visible without scrolling when the homepage first loads.

"Are they open right now?" is the single most common question a restaurant site has to answer, and answering it in four seconds is the difference between a walk-in and a Google Maps redirect to a competitor. Hours belong in the hero, in a persistent header, or in a live "Open · until 10" chip. "See our full hours →" on a link is not a substitute; most visitors won't click.

Related: NAP consistency Related: Google Business Profile

Copyable address #

An address that is selectable, copyable page text — not a baked-in image of an address.

If a visitor can't long-press the address on their phone and hit Copy, they can't paste it into Google Maps, an Uber, or a text to a friend. Images look tidy in a designer's mockup and break every one of those flows. Wrap the real text in a Google Maps link (https://maps.google.com/?q=…) so a tap also launches directions.

Related: directions link Related: NAP consistency

tel: link · tap-to-call #

A phone number wrapped in an HTML anchor with the tel: URI scheme so a phone tap launches the dialer.

The anchor form is <a href="tel:+12125551212">(212) 555-1212</a>. Use the full international format (+1-prefixed) so the same markup works for visitors roaming abroad. A plain-text phone number forces visitors to memorize or copy-paste, losing ~15% of takeout orders and reservation calls at the margins.

<a href="tel:+12125551212">(212) 555-1212</a>
Related: NAP consistency

Cuisine pitch #

One sentence under the restaurant name that tells a first-time visitor what you serve, who you serve, and why they should care.

A first-time visitor should be able to classify your restaurant by cuisine, vibe, and neighborhood in about four seconds. "Wood-fired Neapolitan pizza and natural wine in Silver Spring" does that job. "Welcome to our restaurant" does not. The pitch lives as a subhead beneath the restaurant name in the hero, and re-appears in your <title> tag and og:description meta so the same sentence does the same job in search results and social previews.

Related: title tag Related: JSON-LD schema

NAP consistency · Name, Address, Phone #

Your name, address, and phone number reading identically across your website, Google Business Profile, Yelp, and every directory that links to you.

Google uses NAP matching as a local-search signal; Yelp and Apple Maps use it for directory confidence. When "Joe's Pizza" on the site reads "Joes Pizza" on GBP and "Joe's Pizzeria" on Yelp, aggregators treat them as three different businesses and rank none of them well. The fix: pick one canonical spelling, update every listing, and publish the canonical values in JSON-LD schema so every crawler reads them from one source of truth.

Related: Google Business Profile Related: JSON-LD schema

Dietary markers · allergen callouts #

Per-dish labels — V for vegan, GF for gluten-free, DF for dairy-free, plus halal, kosher, and nut-free callouts — that let dietary-restricted visitors self-qualify the menu.

About 1 in 3 diners avoids at least one food group. When menu items carry visible symbols or a legend, those diners can book with confidence; without them they call to ask, or (more often) choose a competitor whose menu said so plainly. For bakeries, custom-order intake forms should carry an explicit dietary-restriction field because cross-contamination is a food-safety question, not a marketing one.

Audit check: Dietary markers

Mobile experience

~70% of restaurant traffic is on a phone. These are the technical rules a site has to follow to be usable in one hand, in a moving elevator, with the sun on the screen.

Viewport meta tag #

A one-line <meta> tag in the HTML <head> that tells mobile browsers to render the page at phone width instead of zoomed-out desktop width.

Without it, every mobile browser renders a page at ~980px and scales it down to fit the screen — everything looks tiny, nothing is tappable, and visitors bounce instantly. It is the single most catastrophic missing tag a restaurant site can ship, and the cheapest fix.

<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1">
Audit check: viewport

Page speed · Core Web Vitals #

How fast your homepage becomes usable on a phone on a normal mobile connection — measured by Google's Core Web Vitals (LCP, INP, CLS).

Google's own research: 53% of mobile visitors abandon a page that takes longer than three seconds to load. The three headline metrics:

  • LCP (Largest Contentful Paint) — how quickly the biggest above-the-fold element paints. Under 2.5s is good, over 4s is poor.
  • INP (Interaction to Next Paint) — how quickly the page responds to a tap. Under 200ms is good, over 500ms is poor.
  • CLS (Cumulative Layout Shift) — how much the page visibly shifts as it loads. Under 0.1 is good, over 0.25 is poor.

The audit runs a real Lighthouse scan and reports the actual field numbers rather than a vague score.

Audit check: performance Tool: pagespeed.web.dev ↗

Tap targets · 44×44 pixel minimum #

Any button, link, or form control that a phone visitor might tap needs to be at least 44×44 CSS pixels so a thumb can hit it on the first try.

44px is Apple's published human-interface minimum; Google recommends 48px. Smaller targets produce fat-finger misses, and on a restaurant site the most-tapped buttons are usually "Reserve," "Order Online," and the phone number — precisely the surfaces where a miss costs revenue. Pad links generously and give action buttons their own row; don't cram them into dense nav.

Audit check: tap-targets

Body font-size · 16px minimum #

Body copy needs to render at 16px or larger on phones. Anything smaller forces iOS Safari to zoom on input focus and generally reads as tiny.

16px is the baseline. For a restaurant site specifically, menu descriptions and wine lists deserve 17-18px because those paragraphs are the ones convincing a guest to book — they shouldn't require a squint. Use relative units (1rem, 1em) in CSS, not fixed px, so the site respects the visitor's browser settings when they've bumped it up for readability.

Audit check: font-size Related: WCAG contrast

WCAG contrast · WCAG 2.1 AA #

The color contrast standard that makes text readable outdoors, for older eyes, and for visitors with color-vision differences. Body text needs at least a 4.5:1 contrast ratio against its background; large headings can go down to 3:1.

Low-contrast menu text is the #1 design aesthetic that kills conversion on restaurant sites. Thin grey serif on cream looks refined on a designer's laptop and disappears in direct sunlight. Roughly 1 in 4 adults over 40 has some form of age-related vision change; that's the exact demographic that dines out most. Aim for 4.5:1 body text, 3:1 headings, and test outdoors in bright sun before shipping.

Audit check: color-contrast Tool: WebAIM contrast checker ↗

Conversions

The site's job is to turn a curious visitor into a reservation, an order, or directions to your door. These entries cover the surfaces that actually do that.

Reservation CTA #

A "Reserve a table" action visible on every page — in the main nav, in a sticky mobile footer, or both — that launches your reservations flow in one tap.

Fine-dining and casual sites lose reservations when the CTA is only on the homepage. A visitor reading the menu on an inside page still needs a one-tap path to book. Link directly to Resy, Tock, SevenRooms, OpenTable, or an embedded widget — skip interstitials. For subtypes where reservations aren't primary (fast-casual, pizzeria, food-truck), keep the nav space for "Order Online" instead.

Audit check: conversions Related: order online link

Direct-order link · "Order Online" button #

A homepage button that jumps straight to YOUR checkout on Toast, Square, ChowNow, BentoBox, or your POS — not to an aggregator, and not to an interstitial "choose how to order" page.

Every order placed through DoorDash, Grubhub, or Uber Eats takes 20-30% in commission. Every order placed through your own Toast or ChowNow checkout keeps the full margin AND gives you the customer email. For pizzerias specifically the audit treats this as the #1 revenue lever — a direct-order flow vs. routing every pie through Slice is the difference between a ~78% margin and ~55%.

Audit check: conversions Related subtypes: fast-casual, pizzeria, ghost-kitchen

Directions link #

A tappable address or "Directions" button that launches Google Maps or Apple Maps with your restaurant pre-filled as the destination.

Plain-text addresses make the visitor copy, switch apps, and paste — three extra steps that cost you first-time visitors, especially on dim side streets. A https://maps.google.com/?q=… link works on every platform; on iOS it opens Apple Maps by default, on Android it opens Google Maps. Bonus: on a desktop browser it opens web Maps, so the same markup serves both surfaces.

Audit check: platform/maps Related: copyable address

Food photography · real photos, not stock #

Genuine photos of your room, your food, and your people — shot in the actual space, ideally by a professional.

Stock photography reads as dishonest from the first glance. Grainy phone pics read as amateur. The fix is usually a single two-hour shoot with a local food-and-interior photographer once a year — maybe $800-1500 and you have the entire season's marketing assets. For bakeries the custom-cake gallery carries real weight; for bars, the room at golden hour converts more than the cocktail close-up; for food trucks, candid service shots beat styled plate shots.

Audit check: photo-coverage

Gift-card checkout #

A visible purchase flow for physical or e-gift cards, usually powered by your POS (Toast, Square) or a drop-in provider (Yiftee, GiftUp).

Gift cards are the highest-margin line on a restaurant site. Roughly 20-30% of cards go unredeemed — pure revenue — and every redeemed one brings in the recipient plus whatever they spend above the face value. For bars and fine-dining sites especially, a well-surfaced "Gift cards" link in the nav pays for itself every November-December. Square and Toast make this a no-dev-work checkbox once you're on their POS.

Audit check: gift-cards Related subtypes: fine-dining, bar/pub, bakery

Newsletter capture · email signup #

An email-input form that adds visitors to your owned marketing list — typically via Mailchimp, Klaviyo, ConvertKit, or Constant Contact.

Your email list is the only marketing channel you own outright. Instagram's algorithm can bury you, Google's rules can change, aggregators can raise commissions — but a list of guests who said "yes" still delivers. Restaurants with a newsletter capture typically see 3-5× higher repeat-visit rates from subscribers vs. non-subscribers. The form should be inline on the homepage AND footer, NOT a modal popup (they're annoying and convert worse than inline placements).

Audit check: email-capture

Catering / private-events page #

A dedicated landing page for corporate catering, weddings, private dining, and buyouts — with packages, minimums, lead time, and a quote-request form.

Event planners comparing three caterers on a Tuesday afternoon do not read homepages — they search "catering [neighborhood]" and land directly on catering pages. Without one, you rank for nothing and convert nothing; with one, a single holiday-party booking can pay for the catering-menu shoot. For fine-dining restaurants the private-dining room is the margin engine of a slow week. For bars, private-party bookings via Tripleseat are high-ticket, repeat business.

Audit check: catering-page Related subtypes: catering-only, fine-dining, bar/pub

Social proof · press, awards, reviews #

Visible credibility signals — a review quote, a press mention, a Michelin star, a James Beard nomination, a chef bio — placed above the fold rather than buried on a testimonials page.

A first-time visitor is trying to answer "is this place any good?" in about four seconds. A single quoted line from Eater or the NYT does that job faster than any carousel. Restaurants with hero-fold social proof convert skeptical visitors at measurably higher rates. For fine-dining sites, a chef bio block (with a real photo) is itself social proof — it says "there is a human accountable to what comes out of this kitchen."

Audit check: social-proof

Findability

Most restaurant discovery happens in Google Maps and "restaurants near me" searches. These entries cover the structured signals Google reads from your site to render rich listings and rank you locally.

Google Business Profile · GBP, formerly Google My Business #

Your free Google listing that shows up in Google Maps, in the "restaurants near me" panel, and in the sidebar of branded search results.

Roughly 60% of restaurant discovery happens inside Google Maps, not on the open web. A claimed, verified, fully-filled Google Business Profile — correct hours, current photos, accurate category, menu link, reservation link — often drives more traffic than the website itself. Claim yours at google.com/business. Consistency with the website's NAP matters; Google uses matching to validate the listing and rank it locally.

Audit check: GBP lookup Related: NAP consistency, hours accuracy

Title tag #

The HTML <title> element that shows up as the headline of your Google search result and the label on every browser tab.

Rewrite it to include restaurant name, cuisine, and city, under 60 characters so Google doesn't truncate it. A great title tag reads as a human sentence, not keyword soup: "Joe's Pizza — Neapolitan Pizza in Silver Spring" beats "Pizza | Italian | Restaurant | Silver Spring MD." Write one unique title per page; boilerplate "Restaurant Name - Home" everywhere tells Google there's no unique content to index.

<title>Joe's Pizza — Neapolitan Pizza in Silver Spring</title>
Audit check: SEO Related: cuisine pitch

JSON-LD schema · structured data, Restaurant schema #

A hidden <script type="application/ld+json"> block in the page that declares your business to Google in a structured, machine-readable format. For restaurants the key types are Restaurant, FoodEstablishment, Bakery, CafeOrCoffeeShop, and BarOrPub.

Sites with proper Restaurant schema earn rich results — the expanded listing with hours, price range, photos, and menu snippets that sits above plain blue links. Sites without it get the plain blue link. The difference in click-through rate is meaningful. The fields Google actually uses: name, address, telephone, openingHoursSpecification (all seven days), servesCuisine, priceRange, acceptsReservations, hasMenu. Validate with Google's Rich Results Test ↗.

<script type="application/ld+json">
{
  "@context": "https://schema.org",
  "@type": "Restaurant",
  "name": "Joe's Pizza",
  "address": { "@type": "PostalAddress", ... },
  "servesCuisine": "Neapolitan Pizza",
  "priceRange": "$$"
}
</script>
Audit check: schema Related: NAP consistency, hours accuracy

Alt text · image alt attribute #

A short text description of an image written in the HTML alt="" attribute. Screen readers read it aloud; Google Images indexes it; browsers show it when an image fails to load.

For restaurant sites alt text does double duty: it's an accessibility requirement AND a meaningful Google Images ranking signal. Replace filenames like IMG_2041.jpg with real sentences: "Cast-iron rockfish with heirloom tomatoes at Joe's, Silver Spring." Name the dish, the technique, and where appropriate the restaurant and city. Purely decorative images (background flourishes, dividers) should use alt="" — the empty attribute tells screen readers to skip them.

Audit check: photo-coverage

Sitemap.xml · XML sitemap #

A list of every URL on your site that Google should crawl, served at /sitemap.xml and registered in Google Search Console.

Google will eventually find most pages on its own, but a sitemap accelerates indexing for new pages (a just-launched catering page, a blog post, a seasonal menu) and reminds Google of pages it might otherwise skip. Generate one at build time, upload to the site root, verify your domain in Google Search Console ↗, and submit the sitemap URL inside Search Console.

Audit check: SEO

Hours accuracy · site vs GBP vs schema #

Whether the hours on your site, in your Google Business Profile, and in your openingHoursSpecification schema all say the same thing.

Drift happens constantly: you update GBP after Daylight Saving, but your site still says the old hours; you publish a holiday-hours banner in one place but not the other. Google trusts GBP first, so a mismatched site can trigger a "hours may vary" hint in search results — which tanks click-through. For Google Rich Results, schema's openingHoursSpecification needs an entry for every day of the week (including explicitly-closed days) or Google falls back to the hedged fallback. The audit tool cross-checks all three sources and flags mismatches.

Audit check: hours-accuracy Related: GBP, JSON-LD schema

Trust

The small signals that tell a visitor your site is current, secure, and run by humans who read their inbox. Absent these, a site can be technically perfect and still feel off.

HTTPS · SSL / TLS #

Encrypted HTTP. Modern browsers flag plain-HTTP sites as "Not secure" in the address bar, which instantly destroys trust.

HTTPS is non-negotiable in 2026 — it's a Google ranking signal, a prerequisite for modern browser features (geolocation, service workers, the Web Share API), and a user-trust baseline. Free certificates via Let's Encrypt (managed by your host) or Cloudflare (automatic when you proxy through them) mean there's zero cost reason not to. If your host doesn't offer free HTTPS, change hosts.

Transparent menu pricing #

Every dish on the menu shows its price, or (for tasting-menu formats) the total prix-fixe cost is stated prominently.

Hidden prices ("Market price" for every entrée, "Ask your server" across the board) lose first-time diners before they ever book. The exception is legitimate market-price fluctuation (whole fish, seasonal specialties) where listing a range like "$38-52 market" beats "MP." For tasting menus state the total and what it covers — diners comparing "$165 / seven courses" to a $285 counterpart are making a useful decision. Opaque pricing just makes them bounce.

Freshness signal · last-updated / seasonal content #

Any visible indicator that the site is actively maintained — a "Spring menu" badge, a "this week" specials block, a dated blog post, a footer last-updated line.

Stale sites read as closed. A visitor who lands on your homepage in May and sees a "Happy New Year" hero from January assumes you shuttered in between. Cheapest fix: a dated specials block in the hero that staff can update weekly. Next cheapest: a "menu updated [date]" line near the menu link. For seasonal restaurants, a "Summer menu — through September" hero banner simultaneously answers "are you open?" and signals current operation.

Working contact form #

A contact or inquiry form that actually delivers submissions to an inbox someone reads daily, tested end-to-end at least monthly.

Broken forms are common and costly: a visitor fills in a catering inquiry, hits Submit, gets a confirmation page, and the message vanishes into a misconfigured webhook. Test your own form with a real message at least once a month; include a field (like "How did you hear about us?") whose answer will be obvious when you receive it. For high-value forms (catering, private events, press inquiries) consider a fallback mailto: link for visitors whose company firewalls block form submissions.

Restaurant subtypes

The audit tool tailors scoring and copy to ten segments. Each subtype maps to schema.org types, platform hints, and a specific set of priority-check weights — a pizzeria cares about direct-order commission in ways a fine-dining tasting-menu room doesn't, and vice versa. These definitions explain what each segment covers and when it applies.

Fine-dining #

Tasting-menu or prix-fixe restaurants where the reservation is the conversion, the wine list is marketing, and OpenTable/Resy/Tock integration defines the booking funnel.

The audit weighs reservations at 2.0× for fine-dining and the private-dining page at 1.5× — a single corporate holiday buyout pays for a slow week. Common platform fingerprints: Resy, Tock, SevenRooms, OpenTable. Keyword signals: "tasting menu," "prix fixe," "chef's counter," "sommelier," "degustation," "Michelin," "omakase."

Schema: Restaurant

Casual / full-service #

Sit-down restaurants that live in both worlds — reservations for dinner rushes, online ordering for lunch takeout. Neighborhood spots, family-friendly rooms, full-service bistros.

Missing either reservations or direct ordering sends revenue to OpenTable, DoorDash, or a competitor with both. The audit weighs conversions at 1.5× and expects both flows. Common platforms: OpenTable, Yelp Reservations, Toast, Square. Keyword signals: "dining room," "full bar," "lunch and dinner," "signature dishes," "family-friendly," "neighborhood spot."

Schema: Restaurant, FoodEstablishment

Fast-casual / quick-service #

Counter-service restaurants where online ordering IS the business model. Every order through DoorDash/Grubhub costs 20-30% commission; every order through your own Toast or ChowNow keeps the margin.

The audit weighs conversions at 2.0× and menu-format at 1.5× — fast-casual menus ARE the conversion page; prices, item names, and the one-tap Order button need to live together. Platform fingerprints: Toast, ChowNow, Square, BentoBox, Popmenu, Slice, Menufy, Olo, Lunchbox. Keyword signals: "order online," "pickup," "curbside," "grab and go," "fast-casual," "counter service."

Schema: FastFoodRestaurant

Café / coffee shop #

Coffee-led spots where morning traffic is 80% of revenue and the two most-read pieces of content are hours and location.

The audit prioritizes hours visibility, a tappable phone, and a legible menu; reservations are irrelevant, heavy online-ordering flows are nice-to-have but secondary. Platform fingerprints: Square (dominant for café POS), Toast, ChowNow. Keyword signals: "espresso," "cappuccino," "pour-over," "coffee shop," "single-origin," "cold brew." Roasters and bakeries with strong coffee programs usually classify here too.

Schema: CafeOrCoffeeShop

Bakery / pâtisserie #

Bakeries, patisseries, and dessert shops where custom-cake intake and wholesale accounts are the margin engine beneath the walk-in counter.

The audit weighs a wholesale / custom-orders flow at 2.0× for bakeries and treats allergen markers as a trust-and-safety signal, not just a marketing one — a customer ordering a wedding cake for a nut-allergic child needs to trust the cross-contamination policy before they book. Platform fingerprints: Square, Toast. Keyword signals: "pastries," "croissants," "sourdough," "patisserie," "custom cake," "wedding cake."

Schema: Bakery, IceCreamShop

Bar / pub / brewery #

Bars, pubs, taprooms, breweries, wine bars, cocktail rooms — drink-led spots where private-party events and happy-hour clarity drive conversion.

The audit adds an age-gate check (weight 2.0× for bar-pub, 0 for every other subtype) and treats the private-events page as revenue-critical. Cocktail and draft lists rotate constantly, so the HTML menu check runs heavier here too. Platform fingerprints: Tripleseat for events, OpenTable/Resy/SevenRooms for reservations. Keyword signals: "cocktails," "craft beer," "on tap," "happy hour," "gastropub," "taproom," "speakeasy," "brewery."

Schema: BarOrPub, Brewery, Winery, Distillery

Pizzeria #

Pizza-first restaurants where ordering IS the business and every Slice/DoorDash/Grubhub pie costs 20-30% in commission.

The audit weighs conversions at 2.0× and adds a delivery-radius check at 1.5×. A direct-order flow (Toast, ChowNow, Square, or Slice Direct) vs. routing everything through aggregators is the difference between ~78% and ~55% gross margin on a pie. Platform fingerprints: Slice is the strongest tell, then Toast, ChowNow, DoorDash, Grubhub. Keyword signals: "pizza," "neapolitan," "wood-fired," "coal-fired," "sicilian," "detroit-style," "calzone."

Schema: Restaurant, FastFoodRestaurant

Food truck / pop-up #

Mobile kitchens and pop-ups where the primary content question is "where are you today?" — and static storefront maps are worse than no map at all.

The audit weighs the food-truck schedule page at 2.0× for this subtype and downweights maps because a pinned home address is the wrong answer — a dynamic "today's location" block, an Instagram feed, or a schedule calendar is what customers actually need. Reservations are irrelevant; catering inquiries are the margin-rich lead flow. Keyword signals: "food truck," "today's location," "this week's schedule," "pop-up," "catch us," "mobile kitchen."

Schema: Restaurant, FastFoodRestaurant

Ghost kitchen / delivery-only #

Virtual brands and cloud kitchens that operate exclusively through aggregators — no dine-in, no pickup window, customers never visit.

The audit weighs the "explicit delivery-only marker" check at 2.0× for this subtype; without a clear "virtual kitchen / no dine-in" banner, customers show up in person to an empty address and leave one-star reviews. Aggregator links (DoorDash, Uber Eats, Grubhub, Caviar, Deliveroo) are the primary conversion; static storefront maps aren't useful. Keyword signals: "ghost kitchen," "virtual kitchen," "delivery only," "cloud kitchen," "no dine-in."

Schema: Restaurant, FastFoodRestaurant

Catering-only / private events #

Businesses that exist primarily to serve off-premise events — corporate catering, wedding catering, private-chef work, drop-off catering, pop-up dinners.

The entire site IS the catering page; the audit weighs that check at 2.5× for this subtype. Event planners comparing vendors search "catering [neighborhood]" and land directly on catering pages — packages, per-head pricing, minimum lead time, and a structured quote-request form are the critical surfaces. Platform fingerprints: ezCater, CaterTrax, Tripleseat. Keyword signals: "catering menu," "private events," "corporate catering," "wedding catering," "drop-off catering," "request a quote," "off-premise."

Schema: FoodEstablishment, Restaurant
