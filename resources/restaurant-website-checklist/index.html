The Restaurant Website Checklist.
Twenty-three things your site should do in 2026.

A free, honest audit for independent restaurant websites. Run it on your own site in about ten minutes and see where you're leaving reservations on the table. Written for owners, not developers.

By Muntin Digital · Silver Spring, MD

Most independent restaurant websites fail quietly. Not with a bang — with a phone tap that doesn't call the restaurant, a menu that won't open on an iPhone, a reservation button that lands on a broken form. Nobody complains; they just go somewhere else. This checklist walks you through the twenty-three checks I run first on every restaurant site I'm asked to audit. Score your own — 1 point for each "yes" — and see where you stand at the end. It takes about ten minutes.

The basics.

Category 1 of 5 · 5 items
01

Your menu opens on a phone without zooming.

Why
More than 70% of restaurant traffic is mobile. PDF menus are the single most common sin — they render too small to read, and first-time diners bounce within five seconds.
10-sec test
Open your site on your phone. Tap "Menu." Can you read the first dish name without pinching?
Fix
Replace PDF menus with real HTML menu pages. If you must serve a PDF, at least embed it behind a mobile-friendly menu summary so visitors see something readable first.
02

Your hours are visible within 5 seconds of landing.

Why
"Are they open right now?" is the most common reason someone lands on a restaurant homepage. If they can't answer it in five seconds, they call a different place.
10-sec test
Load your homepage. Count the seconds until you see today's hours without scrolling.
Fix
Put hours in the hero, in a persistent header bar, or — best of all — in a small "OPEN NOW · until 10pm" badge that updates automatically based on the current time.
03

Your address is real, copyable text — not an image.

Why
People long-press the address to copy it into Apple Maps or Google Maps. Images aren't copyable, so they have to retype — and half of them don't.
10-sec test
Long-press your address on your phone. Does a "Copy" option appear?
Fix
Use real text wrapped in a Maps link: <a href="https://maps.google.com/?q=Your+Address">. Bonus points for an embedded map that opens to directions on tap.
04

Your phone number is a tap-to-call link.

Why
Tap-to-call converts a visitor into a phone call with one gesture. A phone number you have to copy by hand loses most of that intent — and for a Tuesday-night reservation, the tap-to-call link is the whole funnel.
10-sec test
Tap your phone number on your mobile site. Does the dialer open immediately with your number pre-filled?
Fix
Wrap the number in <a href="tel:+15551234567">. Free, takes thirty seconds, and the conversion lift is real.
05

Your homepage answers "what kind of restaurant is this" in one sentence.

Why
Visitors who don't already know you need cuisine, neighborhood, and vibe in under four seconds. "Welcome to Joe's" tells them nothing. A first-time visitor who can't classify you in the time it takes to drink a sip of coffee will bounce.
10-sec test
Show your homepage to someone who's never heard of your restaurant. Ask them what kind of place it is. If they hesitate, you fail.
Fix
Add a subhead under your name that names cuisine + neighborhood + vibe. Something like "Neighborhood Italian · Silver Spring · family-run since 1998."

Mobile experience.

Category 2 of 5 · 4 items
06

Your homepage loads in under 2 seconds on 4G.

Why
Every second past two seconds loses roughly 10% of mobile visitors to the back button. Google also ranks fast sites higher in local search, so slow = invisible.
10-sec test
Run your URL through pagespeed.web.dev. Look at the mobile "Largest Contentful Paint" metric. Under 2.5 seconds passes. Over 4 is an emergency.
Fix
Compress hero images to WebP or AVIF. Defer non-critical JavaScript. Remove render-blocking web fonts. If your site is on Wix or Squarespace and still slow, you've hit the template ceiling and it's time for a custom build.
07

Tap targets are large enough to hit with a thumb.

Why
Anything smaller than about 44 × 44 pixels on mobile creates "fat finger" failures. A missed tap is a moment of friction, and enough friction sends people back to Google.
10-sec test
Open your mobile site one-handed. Can you tap every button, link, and menu item with your thumb without zooming or squinting?
Fix
Set a minimum button size of 44px. Add padding around text links. Space nav items out so they aren't stacked within a thumb's reach of each other.
08

Your body copy is legible without pinch-zoom.

Why
Text under 16 pixels forces iOS to zoom on focus and frustrates every mobile reader. The menu description that looks fine on your laptop screen is often unreadable on your customer's iPhone at arm's length.
10-sec test
Open your site on an actual phone — not a desktop "responsive view." Can you read a menu description at arm's length without squinting?
Fix
Set your body font-size to at least 16px. For anything you actually want someone to read — menu descriptions, about copy, the story of your place — aim for 17 or 18px.
09

Your site has a proper viewport meta tag.

Why
Without it, mobile browsers render the page at desktop width and zoom out, breaking every other mobile check on this list in one go. It's a one-line fix that nobody should skip, and some template sites still get it wrong.
10-sec test
View the source of your homepage and search for the word "viewport." If it's missing — or reads width=1024 or similar — you fail.
Fix
Add this to your <head>: <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1">. That's it.

Conversions.

Category 3 of 5 · 5 items
10

A reservation action is visible on every page.

Why
A visitor who has decided to book shouldn't have to hunt for how. If your "Reservations" link lives only in a dropdown menu or at the bottom of the homepage, you're adding friction to the exact moment you want to remove it.
10-sec test
Open any page of your site — the menu, the about, the story. Can you start a reservation in one tap without scrolling?
Fix
Put a reservation CTA in your main nav and a persistent sticky footer on mobile. The button should be visible from the first pixel of every page.
11

Your "Order online" button goes straight to checkout.

Why
Every step between "Order online" and your actual POS checkout leaks conversions. A menu page, a cookie banner, a "choose pickup or delivery" splash — each one costs you orders.
10-sec test
Tap "Order online" from your homepage. Count the steps until you see a real menu item you can add to a cart.
Fix
Link directly to your Toast, Square, ChowNow, or custom ordering URL. Skip the interstitial. If your POS doesn't allow direct checkout links, that's a POS problem worth fixing.
12

Your address has a click-to-get-directions link.

Why
On mobile, "Directions" should open the native maps app with your address pre-filled. Anything less — typing, copy-paste, a broken embed — is a tax on visitors who have already decided to come in.
10-sec test
Tap "Get directions" or your address on your mobile site. Does Apple Maps or Google Maps launch with your restaurant as the destination?
Fix
Link your address to https://maps.google.com/?q= followed by your URL-encoded address. Or use the native maps: protocol for iOS.
13

You have real photos of the room, the food, and the people.

Why
Stock photos feel dishonest. Grainy phone pics signal amateur. Real, in-focus photos of the actual space and plates are the single most powerful conversion element on a restaurant website — they either sell the vibe or kill it.
10-sec test
Scroll your homepage. Count the photos that are (a) of your actual restaurant and (b) sharp enough to look professional.
Fix
Hire a local food and interior photographer for a two-hour session. Typical cost: $300–$700. You'll use those photos on the site, Google Business Profile, Instagram, Yelp, and every menu reprint for years.
14

Social proof is visible above the fold — not buried in a testimonials page.

Why
Social proof only works where the decision happens. A glowing Washingtonian review on your "Press" page helps nobody; the same quote in your hero changes bookings this week.
10-sec test
Load your homepage on mobile. Before you scroll, can you see a review quote, press mention, or star rating?
Fix
Move one or two short quotes into the hero or immediately beneath it. A press logo row or a star rating from Google also works. Rotate them occasionally so regulars don't get bored.

Findability.

Category 4 of 5 · 5 items
15

You have a claimed, verified Google Business Profile.

Why
Roughly 60% of restaurant discovery now happens in Google Maps — the "local pack" of three map results that sits above the regular search results. If you don't have a claimed Business Profile, you're invisible to every hungry stranger searching for "[cuisine] near me."
10-sec test
Search your restaurant name in Google Maps. Is there a claimed listing with correct hours, photos, menu link, and category? Does it show a "claimed" badge or let you edit it?
Fix
Claim yours at google.com/business. Free. Setup takes thirty minutes, verification by postcard or phone takes a week. Then fill in hours, categories, photos, menu URL, and service areas. Post at least once a month.
16

Your <title> tag names both your restaurant and your cuisine.

Why
The title tag is the single strongest on-page SEO signal you have. "Home | Joe's" tells Google nothing. "Joe's Trattoria — Handmade Italian in Silver Spring, MD" tells it exactly what to rank you for.
10-sec test
View the source of your homepage and find the <title> tag. Does it contain your cuisine and your city? Is it under 60 characters?
Fix
Rewrite your title to include restaurant name + cuisine + city. Keep it under 60 characters or Google will truncate it in the search results.
17

You have Restaurant schema markup in JSON-LD.

Why
Schema tells Google your hours, menu, price range, cuisine, and phone number in a structured way it can actually read. That's how Google builds the rich result panel with your star rating, hours, and menu — the thing that sits above the organic search results and earns most of the clicks.
10-sec test
Run your URL through search.google.com/test/rich-results. Look for a "Restaurant" or "LocalBusiness" detection. No detection means no rich result.
Fix
Add a <script type="application/ld+json"> block with a Restaurant schema including name, address, servesCuisine, priceRange, telephone, and openingHours. Copy the skeleton from Google's documentation and fill in the real values.
18

Every important image has meaningful alt text.

Why
Alt text describes images to screen readers — that's the accessibility case. It also tells Google what the image is — that's the SEO case. "IMG_2041.jpg" helps nobody. "A plate of handmade ravioli with sage brown butter at Joe's Trattoria in Silver Spring" helps both.
10-sec test
Inspect each photo on your homepage in your browser's dev tools. Does each <img> have an alt attribute that actually describes the image?
Fix
Rewrite alt text as real sentences. Describe what's in the photo and — where it's natural — name your restaurant and city. Decorative images can have alt="", but nothing should be missing the attribute entirely.
19

You have a sitemap.xml submitted to Google Search Console.

Why
Sitemaps tell Google what pages exist and how often they change. Not strictly required, but a big accelerant for new or small sites — and Search Console is the only place you can actually see which keywords are bringing people to you.
10-sec test
Visit yoursite.com/sitemap.xml. If it 404s, you don't have one. Then log into Search Console and check the "Sitemaps" section.
Fix
Generate a sitemap (most static site generators and CMSes do this automatically; there are also free online tools for handwritten sites). Upload to your site root. Verify your domain in Google Search Console and submit the sitemap URL.

Trust.

Category 5 of 5 · 4 items
20

Your site is served over HTTPS with a valid SSL certificate.

Why
Modern browsers show "Not secure" warnings on any site still running HTTP. Visitors don't read the warning — they just leave. And Google demotes insecure sites in local search.
10-sec test
Load your site and look at the URL bar. Is there a padlock icon? Is the URL https:// not http://? No warning banner?
Fix
If your host doesn't offer free HTTPS (via Let's Encrypt or Cloudflare) in 2026, switch hosts. Immediately. There is no good excuse left.
21

Your menu prices are visible — not "Ask your server."

Why
Hidden prices signal that you're embarrassed about them. First-time diners who can't see what the check will be leave to find a place where they can. Even high-end restaurants benefit from honest prices online — the right customer filters themselves in, not out.
10-sec test
Open your menu page. Can you see the price of every dish without clicking through to a PDF or a separate page?
Fix
Show the prices. If you're prix fixe or tasting-menu only, state the total price prominently. If you're a pub with forty items and weekly rotation, keep the top sellers priced and update them in under a minute.
22

Your site has a visible "last updated" signal — or seasonal content.

Why
Stale sites feel closed. A homepage that hasn't changed since 2019 makes visitors wonder if the restaurant is even still open. A visible date, a seasonal menu, or a "this week" section tells them you're still here.
10-sec test
Look at your homepage. Is there anything — a date, a seasonal badge, an event, a weekly feature — that proves the site was touched in the last three months?
Fix
Add "Seasonal menu · Fall 2026" to your menu header. Keep a small "This week" section on the homepage with a rotating special or event. Or display the last content update date in the footer.
23

Your contact form actually sends emails you actually read.

Why
An unread contact form is worse than no contact form — it's a silent trust-poisoning failure. A private event inquiry that never gets a reply becomes a public Google review that definitely does.
10-sec test
Fill out your own contact form with a real test message. Does it land in your inbox within five minutes? Is that inbox actually checked every day?
Fix
Test it every week. If the form isn't delivering reliably, rip it out and replace it with a mailto: link and your phone number. A working simple thing beats a broken sophisticated thing every time.
Score yourself

How did your
restaurant do?

Add up the checks you passed. One point each, out of twenty-three. Then read the band that matches — it's an honest answer, not a polite one.

0 – 8 passes

Failing

Your website is actively losing you customers every week. Most of these fixes cost nothing but attention, and the ones that do cost something pay back within a month. Start with the basics category — even fixing those five alone will move the needle.

  • Fix items 1–5 this week
  • Claim your Google Business Profile next week
  • Budget a custom rebuild within 90 days
9 – 16 passes

Middling

The basics are mostly in place, but the site isn't earning its keep. You're getting some reservations from the site but leaving more on the table. The missing items are usually in Findability and Trust — the ones that turn a passable site into one that ranks and converts.

  • Audit your findability category this week
  • Add Restaurant schema and alt text
  • Refresh photography if it's over two years old
17 – 23 passes

Solid

You're in the top 10% of independent restaurant websites. Now it's a maintenance game — keep the photos fresh, rotate the press quotes, monitor page speed, update the seasonal menu every quarter. The leverage is in staying disciplined, not rebuilding.

  • Schedule a quarterly review of every item
  • Consider a Care Plan so the site doesn't rot
  • Add a blog or events page for recurring freshness
