Menu pricing, grounded: what 100 ingredients told me.

I stopped guessing at menu pricing and did the work: a per-ingredient read of 100 ingredients, grounded in 61 published studies. Here’s what the research says, what our data shows, and what I’d actually change.

In short

  • I grounded menu pricing in 61 published studies and a per-ingredient read of 100 ingredients. This is the plain-English version — what the research says and what I’d change.
  • Of 100 ingredients, 37 are steady enough to print a fixed price, 19 want a built-in cushion, 7 should float on a market line — and for 37 the read honestly won’t call it.
  • Proteins mostly hold: 9 of the 27 scored steady enough to lock, about the same rate as the basket overall. The meat order isn’t the wild card you brace for.
  • Cost the plate on the edible pound, not the invoice pound. The trim tax ran from 1.14× (mushroom) to 2.16× (citrus).
  • When an input spikes, don’t reprint: the median move cleared in 77 days, three in four by about day 105. Pull a temporary lever and recheck.

I price a menu against inputs that never sit still. The citrus that costs one thing in March costs another by June, the produce order swings while proteins hold steady, and the invoice pound is never the pound that reaches the plate. For years I did what plenty of operators I know do — held a price too long, then reprinted in a panic the week the invoice jumped. This spring I stopped guessing and did the work instead.

I joined our public wholesale-price history with open yield, seasonality, and co-movement data into a per-ingredient read for 100 ingredients, checked it against a 134-ingredient yield reference, and grounded every layer in 61 published studies. Two honesty notes before the numbers. This is a descriptive read, not a forecast — it says how an ingredient behaved against its own baseline, never what it will do next. And it leans on wholesale reference levels, which are not the delivered price on your invoice; it stands on peer-reviewed work without being peer-reviewed itself. Here is what fell out.

The margin is thin — but '90% fail' is a myth

Start with the fear, because it drives bad pricing. The claim you have heard a hundred times — that 90% of restaurants fail in the first year — is a myth. When Parsa and his co-authors went and measured it, first-year failure for independents landed near 26%, and the 90% number traced back to a source that could not produce the data behind it (Parsa et al. 2005). The business is hard enough without a made-up figure setting the panic level.

The margins are genuinely thin. Full-service median pre-tax margins run in the low single digits (National Restaurant Association 2025), so a few points of food cost is the whole game. That is exactly why you cannot reprice every time an input twitches. Changing a printed price carries a real cost, and Mankiw's point was not about paper and ink: even a small friction in changing your posted price makes it rational to leave the price alone after a shock, and that rigidity has outsized effects (Mankiw 1985). The reprint is the least of it — the binding cost is informational and relational, the guest who notices (Blinder et al. 1998).

One more myth to clear before the postures make sense. The wholesale number is not the plate. The commodity price you see quoted is a reference, not the delivered price on your invoice, and it is not even most of what a plate costs: farm establishments received about 15.9 cents of each food dollar in 2023 (USDA Economic Research Service 2023). The rest is labor, freight, packaging, the walk from the field to your kitchen. So when a wholesale reference spikes, your plate cost moves less than the headline does.

Print or float: 37 lock, 7 float, 37 withhold

So which prices do you print, and which do you keep loose? I scored all 100 ingredients into four postures. Thirty-seven earned a lock — steady enough against their own baseline to print a fixed price and leave it. Nineteen earned a cushion — print, but build in a buffer because they drift. Seven earned a float — they move too much to pin to a printed number, so they belong on the special board or a market line. And for 37, the read withheld a posture entirely, because the signal was not clean enough to call. I want to name that last column plainly: the instrument says 'I don't know' 37 times out of 100. That honesty is the reason I trust the other 63.

Here is the part that surprised me. You brace for the meat order — it is the one operators lie awake over. But nine of the 27 proteins scored steady enough to print a fixed price. That is a third of the meat order, and it lands right alongside the basket as a whole, where 37 of 100 ingredients earned that same lock. The lesson I took to my own menu: the meat order is not the special case you brace for. Score each ingredient on its own baseline, because the meat order prints about as often as everything else does.

Price on the edible pound, not the invoice pound

The invoice lies to you, and not on purpose. You pay for the as-purchased pound; the guest eats the edible pound; the gap between them is a tax you pay whether or not you cost for it. In our read the tax ran from gentle to brutal: mushrooms lose the least, so the edible pound costs about 1.14 times the invoice pound, while citrus is the worst at 2.16 times. Cost a plate on the invoice pound and you have under-costed citrus by more than double.

This is not guesswork. The USDA's Food Buying Guide publishes as-purchased-to-served yield tables, and they work as a general purchase-yield reference, not only a school-kitchen tool (USDA Food and Nutrition Service 2024). And the loss is not mostly the prep cook's knife: the USDA's cooking-yield tables show that for cooked proteins, a large share of the loss from as-purchased to edible is moisture and fat driven off in the pan, not trim (Roseland et al. 2017). The edible pound has to account for what the fire takes, not only what the knife takes.

Two moves fall out of this. First, rank your plates on contribution-margin dollars, not food-cost percentage — that is the menu-engineering point, and the lowest-food-cost item is not automatically your most profitable one (Kasavana and Smith 1982). Second, the trim you cost for is also trim you can cut: the widely quoted 14-to-1 return on food-waste work is a multi-sector average, and the restaurant-specific figure is about 7 to 1 (Champions 12.3 2019). Costing the edible pound and cutting the waste are the same project seen from two ends.

The swap that mirrors doesn't hedge

Here is the finding that cost me money before I understood it. When an ingredient runs hot, the instinct is to swap in a cheaper cousin. But 94% of the big moves in our read were idiosyncratic — specific to one ingredient, not a wave lifting a whole category. That has a hard consequence: a substitute that moves in step with the thing you are replacing is no hedge at all. When your primary spikes, the mirror spikes with it, and you have swapped your way into the same problem.

The economics back this up. Pindyck and Rotemberg showed that commodity prices co-move more than shared fundamentals explain — the co-movement is 'excess' (Pindyck and Rotemberg 1990). So two ingredients moving together tells you nothing about whether they are economically linked, and a co-moving swap does not protect you. The backup that actually hedges is the one that moves on its own clock, the one whose chart looks nothing like your primary's. Pick the substitute for its independence, not its resemblance.

Don't reprint on a spike — the median cleared in 77 days

The last finding is the one I reach for most. When an ingredient spikes, the median move in our read cleared in 77 days, and three of four had cleared by about day 105. For the median ingredient, the spike self-resolves inside a quarter. Reprint the menu the week the invoice jumps and you have paid the menu cost (Mankiw 1985) to chase a number that was coming back on its own.

This is why regular prices are sticky, and why the stickiness is rational. Bils and Klenow found the median price spell lasts about 4.3 months including sales (Bils and Klenow 2004). Strip the temporary sales out and Nakamura and Steinsson found regular prices change about half as often, closer to every 8 to 11 months (Nakamura and Steinsson 2008). Firms that Blinder's team interviewed reported repricing once or twice a year, with the physical cost of the change ranking low (Blinder et al. 1998). Prices are sticky because repricing has real friction — the research and the floor agree.

So the move is a temporary lever, not a reprint. Pull the item to a special, tighten the portion, run a substitution, and put a recheck on the calendar near day 105. Two honesty notes on yourself. When the input finally falls, watch that you actually bring the price back down — Peltzman found output prices across 242 markets rise faster than they fall in more than two of every three (Peltzman 2000); the rocket goes up fast and the feather drifts down slow, and your menu is not exempt. And do not assume the guest will not notice: a review of 160 studies found no single food-price elasticity, with food away from home among the most responsive categories (Andreyeva et al. 2010). The printed price is a promise. Keep it steady, and move the temporary levers underneath it.

Key takeaways

  • Ground the menu in the read, not the fear — “90% of restaurants fail in year one” is a myth (first-year failure ran about 26%, Parsa et al. 2005).
  • Score each ingredient on its own baseline: 37 of 100 print, 7 float, and for 37 the instrument honestly won’t call it.
  • Cost the edible pound (1.14× to 2.16× the invoice pound), and hedge with the ingredient that moves on its own clock, not the one that mirrors.
  • When something spikes, pull a temporary lever and recheck near day 105 — the median move cleared in 77 days, and changing a printed price carries a real cost (Mankiw 1985).

Every number here traces to the drawer beside it — our field report, and the peer-reviewed work it stands on. This is a descriptive read of public data as of July 16, 2026, not a forecast of your costs. Check my math.
