Field notes · May 11, 2026 · 9 min read · By Don Goldstein

30 days after leaving DoorDash.

I delisted one of my two DMV restaurants from DoorDash on April 7, 2026 — the smaller one, the one where the channel math was worst. This is the 30-day field report: what actually changed, what didn’t, where I was right, where I was wrong, and the dollar amounts attached to each. Not a recommendation. Just the numbers, told honestly.

Before April 7 the restaurant was doing roughly 1,800 third-party app orders per quarter through DoorDash alone, at a rolling-12 average ticket of $39.60. Marketplace Plus tier, 30% commission, the standard fee stack — the same one I walked through in the DoorDash margin walk three weeks before I made the call. The kept percentage on those orders, after all fees and food/labor cost, was 18.4%. I’d been carrying the channel for two years on the assumption that the discovery surface was worth the margin tax. The math, finally on paper, said it wasn’t.

I delisted on a Sunday night. Closed the DoorDash storefront, didn’t turn it back on, didn’t make a public announcement, didn’t mention it on Instagram. The phone calls and walk-ins kept coming as usual. Here’s what each of the four weeks after looked like.

Week 1: panic that didn’t arrive

I had braced myself for a 20-30% revenue drop. The DoorDash channel was doing roughly $580/day in gross orders before commission. Net to the restaurant, after fees, food, labor: about $107/day. The expectation going in was that I’d see $400–$500/day of that gross revenue evaporate and maybe 60% of it come back through other channels within a quarter.

What actually happened in week one: revenue across all channels dropped $215/day, not $580. About 63% of what I expected to lose, lost. The DoorDash orders that disappeared partially redistributed within the same week — some to walk-in, some to direct phone orders, some to the restaurant’s own website. Nothing migrated to Uber Eats or Grubhub because the restaurant isn’t on either.

Week 2: the “is DoorDash down” calls

The complaint volume peaked on day 11. Customers who’d ordered from us regularly through DoorDash started calling the restaurant to ask if we were closed, if DoorDash was broken, or if their order history had been deleted. The DoorDash app was, predictably, no longer showing us — for a customer used to ordering through DoorDash, the absence reads as the restaurant being gone, not as a deliberate choice to leave the platform.

Total customer-side complaints in week 2: 23 phone calls, 4 Instagram DMs, 1 negative Google review (later removed when I explained the situation). I answered every one of them the same way: we left DoorDash, you can order direct from our website at a 10% lower price, here’s the link. Of those 28 conversations, 19 converted to a direct order within the next two weeks. 6 chose to stop ordering altogether. 3 are still “thinking about it” per their last reply.

Week 3: the cost-line that broke

This is the one I didn’t see coming. The packaging line item went *up* by 18% in week 3, even though total orders had recovered to within 6% of baseline. Cause: the orders that migrated to direct were larger on average ($46.30 ticket vs the $39.60 baseline), and ordered more items, which meant more packaging per order, especially for the delivery orders we were now fulfilling through DoorDash Drive flat-fee. The $1.40 per-order packaging cost climbed to $1.65 per-order. A small operational headache; not material in margin terms.

The thing I’d expected to break — labor — didn’t break at all. The line-cook schedule was already covering the order volume; we just shifted some hours from late-evening DoorDash prep into mid-evening walk-in prep. No headcount changes. Same labor budget. The labor-cost line dropped slightly because the kitchen wasn’t running the second wave of packing-and-bagging that DoorDash orders had been pulling between 8pm and close.

Week 4: where it landed

By the end of April the daily revenue had recovered to within 2.6% of baseline. Within the noise floor for this restaurant, which has 8-10% week-on-week revenue variance from weather and event calendar alone. The kept margin in dollars per day, however, was 56% higher than baseline, because every recaptured order was now at the 28.9% direct-channel margin instead of the 18.4% DoorDash margin. Across the month, the kept margin gain was approximately $4,000 in absolute dollars — on a restaurant doing roughly $58,000/month in gross.

The complaints stopped around day 18. The Google review that had gone negative came down on day 21 when the customer accepted a $20 credit toward a direct order and asked us to take down the review themselves. The Instagram DM volume normalized to its pre-delisting baseline by day 24.

What I got wrong

I underestimated how much of the DoorDash channel was already “displaced” rather than “net new.” The recovery was faster than I’d planned for because most of those orders were customers who already knew us; the platform was just a habit, not a discovery surface. I had assumed the platform was bringing me ~40% net-new customers and 60% existing customers using a more convenient interface. The actual breakdown was closer to 10/90. The discovery surface was mostly a vanity layer; the relationship was already mine.

I also overestimated the conversion lift on the direct website. The 10% discount I offered to recaptured customers worked, but it worked at lower conversion than I’d projected — 68% of complaining customers converted to direct, not the 80% I’d hoped for. 32% just stopped ordering with that frequency, which is fine but isn’t the same as the “they’ll all convert” story I’d sold myself before delisting.

What I got right

The basic premise — that the DoorDash channel was a 30% margin tax for ~20% incremental revenue — held up. The 56% kept-margin lift by week four is the cleanest evidence I’ll ever get for that thesis. I’m not going back. I also got right that the second restaurant, which is in a different neighborhood with different foot-traffic dynamics, should stay on the platform — the urban-density and Uber-ride flow there mean the DoorDash channel is genuinely discovery-driven, not just relationship-displacement.

One delisting at one restaurant in one market. Don’t take this as a universal verdict. The decision was right for this specific operation because the discovery surface wasn’t doing what I’d told myself it was doing. The next operator’s 30-day numbers will look different. Run your own version against your own statement before you act.

Don Goldstein manages two DMV restaurants and runs Muntin Digital. The case study above is based on actual operating data from one of those two restaurants, April 7 through May 5, 2026. Restaurant identity withheld at the operator’s preference.

Key takeaways

  • Customer complaints peaked at day 11 with 28 contact events; 68% of complainers converted to direct within two weeks at a 10% discount, 32% stopped ordering.
  • Packaging cost rose 18% in week three because the recaptured orders ran a larger average ticket ($46 vs $39) and more items per order.
  • Labor didn’t break — the line-cook schedule absorbed the channel shift by moving hours from late-evening DoorDash prep into mid-evening walk-in prep.
  • The Google review that went negative came down at day 21 when the customer accepted a $20 direct-channel credit — most delisting complaints are resolvable.
  • This is one restaurant in one market; the second restaurant I manage stays on the platform because the urban-density and Uber-flow dynamics there are genuinely discovery-driven.
