Playbook · May 11, 2026 · 9 min read · By Don Goldstein

The first 30 days after leaving DoorDash.

If you’re considering delisting your restaurant from DoorDash, the 30 days that follow have a predictable shape. This is the playbook — what to expect each week, which costs flex, which complaints arrive, and what the channel mix usually does. Illustrative ranges, not a case study. The underlying margin math comes from the DoorDash margin walk.

In short

  • If you delist from DoorDash without a discovery replacement, expect a single-digit revenue dip in week one as the platform's marketplace traffic stops; most operators recover most of it by week four as recaptured orders shift to the direct channel.
  • Kept margin per ticket rises because every recaptured order moves from the ~18% kept-rate third-party channel to the ~29% kept-rate direct channel (numbers from the <a href="/library/third-party-delivery-economics/">DoorDash margin walk</a>).
  • The platform is mostly a relationship-displacement surface for established independents — net-new discovery is smaller than the operator usually believes. This piece is a playbook, not a case study; numbers are illustrative ranges from the margin walk.

It’s a Sunday night after the close, and the only thing left on the screen is a toggle. Pause storefront. The operator’s thumb has been over it for a week — ever since the statement came back and the kept rate on the platform tickets read like a number somebody had typed wrong. They press it. The store goes dark on the app. No announcement, no countdown, no email blast. Monday opens like any other Monday, except one channel is gone, and nobody on the floor knows yet what that does to the next thirty days.

I’ve watched that exact pause from across a host stand, and watched the month that follows it. Delisting from DoorDash without a discovery replacement produces a predictable shape: a single-digit revenue dip in week one, most of it recovered by week four, and a kept margin per ticket that climbs because every recaptured order moves from the ~18% kept-rate third-party channel to the ~29% kept-rate direct channel. Those two numbers — 18% kept on the standard Marketplace Plus tier (30% commission) after every fee and food/labor cost, against 29% kept on the same ticket ordered direct — are the whole decision. The side-by-side walkthrough lives in the DoorDash margin walk.

You’re not crazy if that pause has been yours. The case for delisting was never that the platform is bad. It’s that the channel rides on a belief about discovery — that the app is bringing in strangers who’d never have found you — and that belief doesn’t survive contact with your own books. What follows is the month, told in the order it arrives: the Sunday toggle, the four weeks after, and the one misread that makes the whole thing recover faster than the operator braced for.

The case for delisting is not that the platform is bad. It’s that the operator is carrying the channel on a belief about discovery that doesn’t survive contact with their own books.

Week 1: panic that didn’t arrive

The first week is the one the operator loses sleep over before it starts. Everyone braces for a 20–30% revenue drop the morning the channel goes dark. What actually arrives is smaller, because the third-party order volume doesn’t evaporate — it redistributes inside the same week, some to walk-in, some to the phone, some to the website. The drop is real. The surprise is how little of it leaves for good. Hold those two pictures side by side:

What you brace for A 20–30% hole that opens Monday and stays open. The dining room quiet, the phone silent, the month already written off. The fear that delisting was the mistake everyone warned about.
What week one delivers A single-digit dip, not a cliff. Orders re-routing to channels you already own. A wave of “wait, are you closed?” that turns out to be the recapture conversation, not the obituary. Illustrative ranges, anchored to the margin walk.

That gap doesn’t close on its own. Week-one recovery is active work, and the order of operations matters — the highest-converting moves go first, while the customer’s attention is still on the change. None of it needs a new tool, a new vendor, or a new line on the P&L. Here is the week as it actually runs, Monday to Sunday:

Week 2: the “is DoorDash down” calls

Complaint volume usually peaks in the second week. Customers who’d ordered through the platform start calling the restaurant to ask if you’re closed, if the app is broken, or if their order history has been deleted. The platform app is, predictably, no longer showing the restaurant — for a customer used to ordering that way, the absence reads as the restaurant being gone, not as a deliberate choice to leave the platform.

Most of these conversations are recapturable. The script: we left the platform, you can order direct from the website at a lower price, here’s the link. Some convert immediately. Some stop ordering. A handful stay in the “thinking about it” bucket. Budget for a small direct-channel credit ($10–$20) for the loudest complaints — most negative reviews come down once the customer accepts the credit and places a direct order.

From the floor

The second week is the one I’d warn a host stand about, because it doesn’t feel like recovery — it feels like the phone turned on you. The call I heard most was a flat, slightly accusing “are you guys closed?” from a regular who’d only ever known the app. The instinct is to apologize. Don’t. The line that worked was warm and short: “Not closed — we left the platform so we’re not paying their cut, and you can order straight from us for less. Want the link?” Said that way, the complaint becomes the recapture. The ones who stayed annoyed almost always softened once a small credit landed and the next order went through direct.

Week 3: the cost-line that flexes

The unexpected line is usually packaging. Recaptured orders run larger than third-party orders did — bigger tickets, more items per order, more delivery containers per order if you’re fulfilling through a last-mile service like DoorDash Drive flat-fee. Per-order packaging cost climbs even when total order volume is flat. It’s an operational headache, not a margin problem.

The line that usually doesn’t break is labor. Line-cook schedules absorb the channel shift by moving hours from late-evening third-party prep into mid-evening walk-in prep. No headcount changes, similar labor budget. The kitchen stops running the second wave of packing-and-bagging that platform orders pulled between 8pm and close.

Week 4: where it lands

By the end of the month, revenue is usually back within single-digit-percent of baseline — inside the noise floor most independents already see week-on-week from weather, events, and seasonality. The kept margin in dollars per day, however, is materially higher than baseline, because every recaptured order is now at the ~29% direct-channel kept rate instead of the ~18% kept rate on the third-party channel. The shift is what makes the math work; total revenue is lower, but retained dollars per ticket are higher.

The number the month turns on

On a $42 ticket, the kept share moves from 18.4¢ on the dollar to 28.9¢ — an illustrative $4.42 more kept per order, same kitchen, same food cost, same labor. Total revenue can land lower for the month and the restaurant still keeps more, because the swing rides every recaptured ticket. Ranges anchored to the DoorDash margin walk.

Complaint volume usually tapers around the third week. The loudest review or DM normally comes down once the customer accepts a small direct-channel credit and places an order through the website. By week four the message channels are back to their pre-delisting baseline.

What operators usually get wrong

The most common operator misread is how much of the third-party channel was already “displaced” relationship volume rather than net-new discovery. The operator believes the platform is bringing in ~40% net-new customers and 60% existing customers using a more convenient interface. After delisting, most operators discover the breakdown is closer to 10/90 — the discovery surface was mostly a vanity layer; the relationship was already theirs. The recovery is faster than expected because most of those orders were going to come back anyway.

The other common misread is the conversion rate on a recapture offer. A 10% direct-channel discount works, but it converts at a lower rate than operators usually project. Some customers stop ordering at the previous frequency — not catastrophic, but not the “they’ll all convert” story most operators sell themselves before delisting.

What the math gets right

The basic premise — that the third-party channel is roughly a 30% margin tax on what may be a smaller incremental-revenue surface than the operator believes — holds up across most independent restaurants. The kept-margin lift on direct orders is the cleanest evidence available for the thesis. The decision works for restaurants where the channel is mostly relationship-displacement; it doesn’t work for restaurants in genuinely discovery-driven contexts (urban density, transit-corridor flow, tourist neighborhoods) where the platform is actually bringing in new customers.

Thirty days after that Sunday-night toggle, the screen the operator stares at is a different one — their own statement, not the app’s dashboard. The dining room sounds the same. The phone has stopped asking if they’re closed. What changed is invisible from the floor and unmistakable on the page: more of every ticket stays. The pause that felt like a leap was mostly the operator catching up to a number the platform had been keeping quiet. That’s the whole arc — not a dramatic turnaround, but a quiet correction to what was always true.

The 30-day verdict, in three lines. Revenue usually recovers to within single-digit-percent of baseline by week four. Kept-margin-per-day climbs because every recaptured order moves from a ~18% kept rate to a ~29% kept rate. Most operators discover the platform was a relationship lever they’d misread as a discovery lever — the cost of that misread compounds annually until the math gets put on paper.

One playbook isn’t a universal verdict. The decision is right for operations where the discovery surface isn’t actually doing the discovery work the operator thinks it is. The next operator’s 30-day numbers will look different. Run your own version of the margin walk against your own statement before you act.

Key takeaways

  • Plan for customer-complaint contact events in the first two weeks; offering a small direct-channel credit converts most complainers to direct ordering.
  • Packaging cost rises in step with average-ticket size because recaptured orders run larger than third-party orders.
  • Labor often absorbs the channel shift by moving hours from late-evening third-party prep into mid-evening walk-in prep.
  • Most delisting complaints are resolvable with a direct-channel credit; budget for a handful of small offers in the first month.
  • Discovery-driven markets (urban density, transit flow) may keep the platform worth running; channel-by-channel margin math, not a blanket rule.
