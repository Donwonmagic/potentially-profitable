# Don's voice rubric

A one-page lock on how Muntin Digital's editorial voice reads.
Distilled from Don's existing flagship long-form (the menu-prices
playbook, the DoorDash math piece, the reservation-leak post) and
the tool-hero ledes that already hit the mark.

If you're writing new copy — a tool hero, a glossary "Why it
matters" paragraph, a blog post, a knit aside — read this first.
The goal is not to imitate; the goal is to keep the seven moves
below in mind, so the new sentence sounds like the seventeen
sentences around it.

---

## The seven moves

**1. Direct second person.** "You" before "your business." "You're"
before "many operators are." Address one operator standing across
the counter from you, not a category.

> *You don't want to raise prices. Your costs have already gone up,
> your suppliers' costs have already gone up…*

Not: "Restaurant operators face increasing pressure on margins."

**2. Empathy first, then redirect.** Acknowledge the obvious
emotional read of the situation, then move the conversation to a
better question.

> *I understand the fear. I also think the fear is usually solving
> for the wrong risk.*

Not: jumping straight to the prescription.

**3. Concrete numbers with named units.** Always show the math.
Always anchor the percentages to a dollar figure or a real-world
unit (sous chef's salary, a four-top, a cover, a mile).

> *…that four-point drift is your profit margin walking out the
> back door. For an independent restaurant doing $1.2M a year,
> that four points is around $48,000 — more than a reliable sous
> chef's salary, evaporating quietly.*

Not: "significant impact on profitability."

**4. Named tradeoff per claim.** No claim ships without naming what
it costs. If you raise prices 6%, you lose 3% of covers — say so.
If a calculator runs in the browser, the cost is no save-across-
devices unless you sign in — say so.

> *Type your numbers; we do the math in your browser. No sign-up,
> no uploading, no server ever sees what you type.* (margin-math)

The tradeoff IS the trust signal. Hiding the tradeoff hides the
voice.

**5. One vivid sensory anchor per beat.** A picture the reader can
see. A regular opening their phone. A burger price changing. A
menu PDF dropped over the weekend. Sensory specificity is the
difference between a playbook and a press release.

> *…a Monday morning where regulars open their phones, see the
> same burger that was $17 last week now at $19, and quietly book
> somewhere else.*

Not: "customer churn may result."

**6. Anti-pattern → correct-pattern juxtaposition.** Show the wrong
move, name the cost, then show the right move. The reader learns
faster from "here's how this typically goes wrong" than from a
clean recipe.

> *Getting it wrong looks like this: a flat percentage bump on
> every item, a new PDF menu uploaded over the weekend… Getting
> it right looks like three tiers.*

Not: a numbered checklist with no failure mode.

**7. One honest doubt per piece.** Every piece names what's
uncertain, what doesn't generalize, what the author isn't sure
of. The doubt earns the rest of the confidence.

> *These numbers are illustrative, drawn from patterns I see across
> the independent restaurants I work with — not from a published
> dataset. Your own menu will vary.*

Not: false certainty, claimed studies, "research shows."

## Sentence shapes

Mix long observation sentences with very short verdicts. The
short ones land because the long ones earn them.

> *…the covers you lose at the margin were the lowest-spending ones
> anyway. **That's the whole game.** The trick is getting the 6%
> without the 3% becoming 15%.*

The two-word verdict ("That's the whole game") would not work
alone. It works because the previous sentence did the analysis.

---

## Grammar of "we" and "I"

- **"I"** appears when Don is speaking from his own kitchen
  experience or his own restaurants. Never from a generic studio
  voice. "I manage two restaurants and build websites…" is "I."
- **"We"** is Muntin Digital — the studio, the tools, the audit
  pipeline. "We do the math in your browser." "We don't store
  your numbers."
- **"You"** is the reader-operator. Never plural ("you all," "you
  guys"). Never abstract ("the operator," "users").

## Words and phrases to avoid

Industry-stale, abstract, or jargon-without-grounding. If you
catch yourself reaching for one of these, find the concrete
behind it.

| Avoid | Reach for |
|---|---|
| "leverage" | use, run, lean on |
| "robust" | a number that quantifies it |
| "users" / "customers" | diners, regulars, owners, an operator, a guest |
| "best practices" | name the practice, name who tested it |
| "world-class" | nothing — just delete |
| "seamless" | nothing — describe the seam being fixed |
| "thought leader," "transform," "unlock" | nothing |
| "research shows" (without a citation) | "I see this in the audits" / a real study + drawer |
| "businesses" | restaurants, salons, dental clinics — the specific industry |

---

## Words and phrases to keep

Words that recur in Don's writing because they carry meaning
specific to this voice. Not required, but flag worthy:

- "quietly" / "without you noticing" — for things that erode margin
  or trust over time
- "the math" — for any decision that has a number underneath
- "the menu" / "the room" / "the door" / "the guest" — restaurant-
  specific anchors that ground a website point in a kitchen point
- "the playbook" — for a sequence Don actually uses
- "the tradeoff" — names the cost explicitly
- "an operator" — singular, address-able, not a category

---

## Length

- Tool hero H1: ≤ 7 words. Question shape preferred (`Is your
  website mobile-friendly?` / `How fast is your site?`).
- Tool hero lede: 2–3 sentences. Open with the operator's question;
  close with the tradeoff (privacy / time / cost).
- Glossary "Definition": ≤ 30 words. Plain. No jargon.
- Glossary "Why it matters": 60–100 words. Names a number, names a
  tradeoff, names a typical anti-pattern.
- Blog post first paragraph: 3–5 sentences. Empathy first, redirect
  to the better question, signal what the piece will deliver.
- One-line verdicts inside long pieces: keep them ≤ 8 words. Use
  sparingly — once or twice per section.

## Self-test before shipping

Read the piece aloud. If any of these is a "no," rewrite:

- [ ] **Does it sound like a person speaking, or like marketing?**
      A person says "the math stopped working four months ago." A
      marketer says "operational efficiency challenges."
- [ ] **Is there at least one number with units?**
      "$48,000 a year" beats "significantly impacts margin."
- [ ] **Is there one named tradeoff?**
      "Free, no sign-up, no upload" works because the user knows
      what they're trading away (cross-device save).
- [ ] **Is there one honest doubt?**
      "Your own menu will vary" earns the rest of the confidence.
- [ ] **Could a chef read this in five minutes between services?**
      No three-syllable Latin verb when a one-syllable Anglo-Saxon
      noun will do.
- [ ] **Does it survive removing every adjective?**
      If yes: ship it. If the meaning collapses without "powerful"
      and "comprehensive," the meaning was the adjective —
      rewrite around the verb instead.

## Form: interactivity and imagery

Voice is how the words sound. Form is how the words *show*. Both
serve one goal — help an operator learn the thing in the time
they have.

### Interactivity (when appropriate, never forced)

Add an interactive element when it teaches faster than prose alone.
Skip it otherwise. The bar:

> **A reader who can *do* the thing in two clicks understands it
> better than a reader who can *read* the thing in two paragraphs.
> If the doing fits in two clicks, ship the doing. If it doesn't,
> ship the prose.**

Where it belongs (already on the site):

- **Tool pages.** The whole point — paste a URL, see the score.
- **Worked examples in math articles.** Inline calculator that
  prefills with sensible defaults; the reader plugs in their own
  numbers and watches the conclusion change. Better than a static
  worked example because it forces the operator to feel the
  shape of the math.
- **Citation drawer.** Click a chip, the source summary slides in.
  The reader doesn't lose their place in the article.
- **Audio narration.** Read OR listen — the operator picks.

Where it doesn't belong (don't add):

- **Glossary definitions.** The reader is there for a definition.
  An interactive widget is a delay. Keep them text — link out to
  a tool when the term is one ("plate-cost" → /tools/plate-cost/).
- **Hero sections.** The hero answers "what is this page." A
  hero-resident widget pulls focus from the answer.
- **Anywhere a single sentence would do.** "Audit your site in
  thirty seconds" is more useful than a 4-step interactive
  preview of an audit running.

The honest tradeoff: every interactive element is one more thing
that can fail (no JS, slow network, accessibility regressions).
Always provide a working text-only fallback. The four-states
module (`tools/_shared/states.js`) is the standard error/loading
shape — use it.

### Imagery (where possible, never decorative)

A diagram beats a paragraph when:

- The relationship is **spatial** (where buttons live; how a flow
  branches; what's above the fold).
- The relationship is **proportional** (this slice is 30% of that
  one; this number dwarfs that one).
- The relationship is **before / after** (the homepage was this;
  now it's this).

A diagram **doesn't** beat a paragraph when the relationship is
sequential (steps), conditional (if X then Y), or numeric (a
table reads better). Use prose or a table.

Locked imagery patterns on the site:

- **Subject-cue glyphs.** Every tool, every OG card, every blog
  post hero starts with a single SVG glyph (24px stroke-1.75
  line-icon, brand color). The glyph is a one-second "what kind
  of thing is this" cue. Don't invent a new glyph if an existing
  one fits — the registry lives in `scripts/build-og-cards.mjs`'s
  `GLYPHS` block.
- **Status chips.** `.status-chip[data-band="failing|middling|solid"]`
  carries pass/warn/fail vocabulary across every tool. Use it
  when you'd otherwise reach for a colored dot or a custom badge.
- **Progress ring** (`.progress-ring`). The 0–100 score primitive
  on grader pages. Don't recreate the wheel.
- **The muntin-pattern background** (5% opacity grid) on OG cards
  and the audit hero. The brand leitmotif. Never use it at higher
  opacity — it stops being texture and starts being decoration.

Production rules:

- **SVG over PNG** for diagrams and illustrations. Smaller, scales
  cleanly, theme-color responsive.
- **One illustrative example per piece, max two.** Beyond two and
  the page becomes a deck.
- **Always alt-text.** A blind operator should learn the same
  thing a sighted one does. If the alt-text feels redundant with
  the prose, the image was decorative — delete it.
- **No stock photography.** Nothing on the site is generic. A
  stock photo of a smiling barista undermines a piece about real
  margin loss in a real kitchen.
- **Captions are doing work.** A caption that just restates the
  image title is wasted real estate. The caption either names the
  source ("Lighthouse, Apr 2026"), the constraint ("at 3G
  throttling"), or the tradeoff being illustrated.

The honest tradeoff: imagery costs production time, accessibility
review, and bandwidth. Skip it when prose carries the meaning.

---

## How this rubric is used

- **New tool / page**: read this before drafting. The seven moves
  + the length table + the form rules cover the structural
  decisions.
- **Editing existing copy**: read the piece aloud, then run the
  self-test. Rewrite the parts that fail.
- **Adding interactivity or imagery**: pass the bar above first.
  If the answer to "would this teach faster than prose?" is "no
  or unsure," ship prose.
- **Reviewing AI-drafted copy**: AI drafts can pass the seven
  moves on their face but still feel hollow. The two reliable
  tells: (a) every adjective trying to do the work a noun should
  do; (b) no honest doubt. If either is true, send back.

The rubric is intentionally short. If a future edit makes it
longer than two pages, that's a sign the voice has become rules-
shaped instead of person-shaped. Trim it back.
