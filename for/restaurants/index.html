Reservations

A booking button they can tap at 10pm

Most diners decide where to eat from their phone, after hours, in bed. If your only option is "call us tomorrow," you lose them to the next place with a booking button.

  • They decide on their phone. The majority of restaurant decisions start on mobile and most happen outside your operating hours. A phone-only path loses every diner deciding at 11pm.
  • Reminders cut no-shows. Automated email and SMS confirmations with calendar links keep covers booked. One no-show a week at $80 a cover is over $4,000 a year back.
  • It frees your host stand. Every reservation that lands online is a call your FOH doesn't take during rush. Fewer interruptions, faster seating, better service.
  • It drives discovery. OpenTable, Resy, Tock, and Google Reserve all surface you to diners who'd never hit your site directly. Bookings are pull, not just push.
  • Covers flow into your POS. Toast, Square, and Lightspeed all integrate. Your team sees the reservation, the cover count, and special requests without re-keying a thing.

Menu management

A menu you can update from your phone

A PDF menu isn't a menu — it's a snapshot. The version your diners see at 7pm Tuesday should reflect what you 86'ed during lunch.

  • Stale menus break trust. Diners who see last season's prices, or an item you stopped serving in June, assume the rest of the site is just as out-of-date. Many won't book.
  • Google reads live menus, not PDFs. Every dish name on a live HTML menu is a potential search result. "Heritage pork sugo near me" can land on your site. A PDF menu is a black box to search.
  • Changes take seconds. A price tick, a sold-out item, tonight's special. You pull out your phone, type, save. No designer, no ticket, no wait.
  • Screen readers work. Accessible menus serve every diner. Most PDF menus fail accessibility audits; well-built live menus pass by default.
  • Share a dish, not a 12 MB file. Link the rockfish to Instagram, embed tonight's special in your email blast, drop a single URL in your Google Business Profile.

Online ordering

Keep the margin, keep the diner

Third-party delivery apps take a 15–30% bite out of every ticket. Your own ordering path keeps more of every dollar — and, just as importantly, keeps the customer relationship.

  • Third-party fees stack up. DoorDash and Grubhub typically take 15–30% per order. A direct checkout keeps almost all of it. Over a year that's real money — often a line-cook's salary's worth.
  • You own the customer. When DoorDash has the email, DoorDash has the repeat order. When you have the email, you have a relationship you can market back to — specials, seasonal launches, private events.
  • The integration has to fit. Toast, Square, ChowNow, or a direct Stripe checkout — each suits a different kind of restaurant. We pick based on your POS, menu complexity, and staff workflow, not on what's trendy.
  • Speed to checkout is everything. The fastest path is three taps from the menu. Every extra step loses orders. Live menu, one-tap add, saved card. Friction is the enemy.
  • Pickup vs. delivery is a real decision. Self-delivery has margin but operational pain. Third-party delivery has reach but cost. The right mix is different for every restaurant, and we walk you through it instead of defaulting to a setup.

Google Business Profile

Most restaurant traffic is on Google Maps

When someone types "best Thai near me," Google shows the three-pack of Maps results above everything else. If you're not optimized for that pack, you don't exist for those diners.

  • The map pack is everything. Over half of restaurant discovery starts inside Google Maps, not a search results page. If your profile is incomplete or stale, you lose to competitors whose profiles are dialed in.
  • Hours, photos, menu link — in that order. The three highest-ROI fields on GBP. Accurate hours build trust, photos drive clicks, a menu link keeps diners on your page. All three are free to fix.
  • Review response shapes rank. Google's algorithm rewards restaurants that reply to reviews — both positive and negative. It's also the single clearest trust signal new diners check before booking.
  • Category tuning is underrated. "Italian Restaurant" vs. "Italian Trattoria" vs. "Pasta Restaurant" changes which searches you surface for. Most restaurants are set to the wrong primary category and never know.
  • Posts are free real estate. Tonight's special, a seasonal menu drop, an event — GBP Posts surface directly in your listing. Most restaurants ignore them completely; every one you publish is pure upside.

Mobile speed

Under two seconds, or you lose them

Your diner is on a sidewalk, hungry, on 4G. Every extra second of load time loses a measurable share of them to the next listing down the street.

  • The numbers are brutal. Google's research: a one-second delay in mobile load can drop conversions by around 20%. For a restaurant, that's reservations and orders walking to the next listing.
  • Speed is a ranking signal. Google Core Web Vitals factor directly into search rank. Slow restaurants get outranked by faster competitors, even with the same cuisine and the same neighborhood.
  • Images are the usual villain. Most slow restaurant sites are slow because of oversized hero photos. We compress, resize, and lazy-load so your food still looks gorgeous but the page loads in a second.
  • Measured, not guessed. Every Muntin build ships with a PageSpeed score north of 90 on mobile. We run the test before launch and periodically after — speed isn't a one-and-done concern.
  • Fast doesn't mean sacrificed. You don't have to pick between "looks beautiful" and "loads fast." Modern build practices give you both. We just have to actually use them — and most restaurant sites don't.

Food photography

Photos sell the table

Diners decide with their eyes. A menu written in Helvetica can't compete with a photo of the pasta they're about to order.

  • Every dish deserves a photograph. Start with your four or five hero items if you need to. Good photos drive reservation clicks, Instagram shares, and Google Business Profile engagement — the things that feed the top of the funnel.
  • Your space is part of the pitch. Wide shots of the dining room, the bar at golden hour, the kitchen in motion. Diners book with confidence when they can see the room they're walking into.
  • Responsive galleries, no distortion. Photos adapt to phone, tablet, and desktop without warping or cropping in ways you didn't intend. Lazy-loaded so they don't drag page speed down.
  • Bring your own or we'll match you. We work with several DMV food photographers if you need one. If not, good phone photos can go a long way — we can do a lot with solid raw material.
  • Grow the gallery as you go. New special? Shoot it on your phone, upload from the dashboard. Your site grows with your menu instead of frozen at launch day.

Brand system

Your logo is one ingredient, not the recipe

Two restaurants can launch with equally strong marks; six months later the one with a documented brand system looks the same on its menu, its Instagram, and its takeout bag — and the one without it doesn't.

  • Colors are the biggest tone carrier after the logo. A primary, a secondary, a handful of accents, and two neutrals — each with a documented accessible pair. That's what the site renders; that's what print vendors should receive.
  • Contrast failures make menus unreadable. Brand colors that look lovely in the logo can fall well below the WCAG 4.5:1 threshold on a busy menu page. Every pair needs to be graded, not eyeballed.
  • Every surface gets the same token. We ship the palette as CSS custom properties + design tokens so the site, your future dev, and any print vendor all read from the same source.
  • Monochrome variant is not optional. Embroidery, foil emboss, laser-engraved coasters — they need a one-color version of the mark. The absence of one is the single clearest "this identity wasn't finished" tell.
  • Documented usage rules prevent drift. Clearspace, minimum sizes, misuse examples. Owners underestimate how much work a two-page usage guide saves over five years of vendor handoffs.

Questions restaurant owners ask

Straight answers
for restaurant owners.

Which POS systems do you integrate with?

Toast, Square, Clover, Lightspeed, and Revel are the common ones. For online ordering we also work with ChowNow, direct Stripe checkouts, and a few smaller menu-first tools. If you tell me what you're on, I'll tell you honestly whether it integrates cleanly or not.

Can I update my menu myself?

Yes — that's the whole point. You log in from your phone, change a price, add a special, or swap a dish, and the site re-renders in seconds. No code, no tickets, no waiting for a developer.

How do you handle reservations?

OpenTable, Resy, Tock, or Square Reservations embedded cleanly — or a simple built-in form that emails you and your hostess stand. Whatever workflow your team already trusts, we wire up around it instead of replacing it.

What about online ordering?

Two routes. Handoff: a clean button that sends customers to your existing Toast, Square, or ChowNow storefront. Direct: a built-in checkout that runs through your own payment processor so you keep the margin. We help you pick based on your order volume.

Do you help with Google Business Profile?

Yes — setup, photo optimization, hours, menu link, and category tuning are included in every build. Most small restaurants are getting >60% of their new customers from Google Maps, not organic search, so this is usually the highest-ROI hour of the whole project.

How much does a custom restaurant website cost?

Essentials starts at $2,500. Most restaurants land in Full Service at $5,000–$9,000. Multi-location or groups with POS integration needs run $8,000–$15,000+. No hourly billing, no surprise invoices — you see the number before we start.

Do you only work with restaurants in Silver Spring?

No — Muntin is based in Silver Spring, MD, but we work with restaurants across the DMV (Montgomery County, DC, Northern Virginia) and remotely with restaurants anywhere in the US. Local clients get in-person kickoff and photo sessions if they want them.

How long until my site is live?

Essentials launches in 2–3 weeks, Full Service in 4–6, Multi-Location in 6–10. Only two active builds run at a time, so the schedule is real — not a placeholder that slips.
