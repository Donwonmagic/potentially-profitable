I manage Tacombi Bethesda. When we rebuilt the website three quarters
ago, here's what changed and what didn't.

What changed:

  • Reservation conversion went from 2.1% to 4.4%. About half of that
    was deeplinking the OpenTable button instead of dumping people on
    OpenTable's home. The other half was making the menu visible
    above the fold on a phone.

  • Direct online ordering — the kind that doesn't pay an aggregator
    a take — climbed from 12% of digital orders to 31% across the
    next six months. The driver was making "Order direct" the bigger,
    higher-up button, and "DoorDash / Uber" a smaller link below it.

  • Spanish-language reservations roughly doubled. Bethesda's a
    bilingual market and the old site shipped a Google Translate
    plugin instead of a real /es/ mirror. Real localization, real
    impact.

What didn't change:

  • Total covers. The website moved channel mix, not raw demand.
    People who would have walked in still walked in; people who would
    have ordered DoorDash still mostly did. The site closes the gap
    between intent and action — it doesn't create new intent.

  • Kitchen close-time. We're still on the same headcount. The site
    doesn't make the line cook's life easier.

  • Reviews. The website doesn't move review counts; doing right by
    the guest does. The website helps you respond faster.

The point of the case study is that the website is a margin tool, not
a marketing tool. It doesn't bring you new customers — it stops you
from losing the ones you have to friction.
