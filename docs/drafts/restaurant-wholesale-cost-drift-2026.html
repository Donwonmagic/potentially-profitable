Dispatch · June 19, 2026 · 6 min read · By Don Goldstein

The menu is a photograph. The market is the weather.

You priced the chicken sandwich in January. You photographed the cost of a chicken breast on one particular Tuesday, framed it at sixteen dollars, and hung it on the wall. Then you walked away — because that’s what a menu is, a print you stop looking at. The trouble is that the thing you photographed kept moving after the shutter closed.

I wanted to know how far it moves. Not the scary headline number, not “inflation” in the abstract — the actual distance between the price you set and the market you set it against, over the kind of gap most kitchens leave between menu updates. So I went to the Muntin Cost Index’s own history and measured one clean quarter: where each staple’s wholesale level sat in January, where it sat in April, and the line between. No invoices, no operator data. Just the weather, recorded against itself.

The chicken sandwich is the one that stops you. Over that quarter, wholesale boneless skinless breast went from about a dollar twenty-nine a pound to about a dollar seventy. Same recipe, same menu price, same little photo on the wall — and the protein under it had quietly gotten almost a third more expensive to put on the plate.

It isn’t one ingredient. It’s most of them.

If it were only chicken, you’d fix chicken and move on. It isn’t. Across the whole tracked panel that quarter, roughly two out of every three staples moved more than five percent from where they started. The typical item drifted only about three and a half percent — but “typical” hides the story, because the spread was enormous. A quarter of the panel fell six percent or more. Another quarter climbed seventeen percent or more. Wholesale tomatoes more than tripled on their way into spring; eggs fell by about a third as the flock came back.

That dispersion is the whole trap. A five-percent move on one line is invisible inside a menu you set in winter. You don’t notice any single shutter-click of the weather. You notice the season — later, as a month that came in thinner than it should have, with no one decision you can point to.

Don’t reprice more. Re-develop the photo.

The instinct after reading any of this is to reach for the whole board and bump every price a dollar. Resist it — a board-wide bump to cover one ingredient just teaches your regulars to read prices, and it punishes the dishes that didn’t move. The skill isn’t repricing more often. It’s knowing, plate by plate, which photographs the market has aged past their frame, and re-developing only those.

That’s the whole reason the free Plate Cost tool now recosts your saved recipes against the live Cost Index and flags the dishes that slipped: it re-develops the photo for you, against this week’s light. A menu is a photograph of a market that has already left the room. You can’t make the weather hold still for the print. You can only decide how long you’re willing to keep selling January’s prices in April.
