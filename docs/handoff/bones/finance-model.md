## 0. The headline: the verdict's own ARR figure is internally inconsistent

The strategy states the price as **"$600/location/month, billed annually at $6,000"**
and, three sentences later, **"40 x $600 = $288K ARR"**.

| | arithmetic | ARR at 40 locations |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly list price | 40 x $600 x 12 | **$288,000** |
| The price actually invoiced | 40 x $6,000 | **$240,000** |

$6,000/yr is $500/month. The annual prepay is a **16.7% discount that was never
named as a discount**, and the headline ceiling is overstated by $48,000/yr — 20%.
Either the annual price is $7,200, or the ceiling is $240K. Both cannot be true.
Everything below models **$6,000/location/year** because that is the number a
customer would actually be invoiced, and shows list-price ARR alongside it.

## 2. The founder-hour engine — the real cost driver

The verdict computes one obligation: `~20 founder-minutes of close review x 40
locations = 13.3 h/month against 13-26 available`. That is close review **only**.
A close that is signed, sold, invoiced and supported carries four more loads the
verdict never adds:

| Load | Why it exists | Verdict budgets it? |
|---|---|---|
| Close review, per location | the signature | **yes**, 20 min |
| Exception-desk RULE review, per location | the desk authors rules; a signature means the founder read them | no |
| Support / relationship / invoicing / collections, per customer | hand-invoiced, no billing code, no dunning | no |
| Platform upkeep | 306 cost-index pages kept fresh, 21 manual `(idem)` builders, agent-PR review, keeping the deploy green | no |
| Acquisition + onboarding | 8-12 specialty vendors per customer, each layout needing real invoice A + held-out invoice B | 3 h/win, and no onboarding at all |

## 5. Unit economics of a hand-delivered close

Founder hours valued three ways. **BLS national median for food service managers is
$65,310 (May 2024)**; a Maryland median of $88,470 appears in commercial salary data
(EXTERNAL, weaker source). $45/h is the base; $30 and $75 bracket it.

| Case | Locations at ceiling | Founder h/mo | Revenue | Founder cost @$45/h | Residual to founder | Cost per location-month | Gross margin |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| SLOW | 3.6 | 13.0 | $21,600 | $7,012 | **$224** | $495 | 1.0% |
| BASE | 19.8 | 20.0 | $118,800 | $10,814 | **$93,622** | $106 | 78.8% |
| FAST | 51.4 | 26.0 | $308,400 | $14,034 | **$280,002** | $46 | 90.8% |

**The spread is the whole finding.** The same strategy, the same price, the same buyer,
produces **$224/year** or **$280,000/year** depending on one number the company has
never measured. That is not a range of outcomes; it is a range of businesses.

**Founder time becomes the binding cost immediately — infrastructure is never binding.**
At $45/h the founder's own time is 33% of total cost at the FAST ceiling and **97% at
the SLOW ceiling**. Infrastructure crosses $1,197/month at zero customers and barely
moves; two locations pay for all of it forever.

### Income replacement

Replacing a $65,000-$88,000 W-2 requires more than $65,000-$88,000 of business revenue:
self-employment tax adds the employer half (~7.65% on net SE income up to the wage base),
and individual health coverage that Tacombi presumably provides today is a new expense
(ASSUMED $5,400-$9,600/yr — I could not verify a Maryland marketplace premium).
Call replacement **$95,000-$120,000 of pre-tax business residual**.

| Case | Residual at service ceiling (@$45/h founder cost added back = full residual) | Replaces the job? |
|---|---:|---|
| SLOW | $7,236 | **no** — not remotely |
| BASE | $104,436 | **marginally** — bottom of the band |
| FAST | $294,036 | **yes** |

### Does quitting ever become rational? No — and that is structural, not incidental.

At the BASE ceiling the business clears roughly $104,000 pre-tax **on 20 founder-hours
a month**. Quitting frees ~140 additional hours a month into a business whose revenue
ceiling is set by a **policy cap of 40 locations**, not by capacity. Even in the FAST
case, 40 locations consume 21.3 of 26 available hours; a full-time founder could serve
~150 locations and is permitted 40.

**The strategy is engineered to be servable in 13-26 hours a month, which is exactly
what makes it structurally unable to ever become the founder's job.** Quitting converts
a 79-92% margin side business into a full-time job at identical revenue. The only way
the model reaches escape velocity is by lifting the 40 cap — which requires a channel
that produces more than 40 customers, and the verdict killed every channel it examined
(the bookkeeper channel, traffic, list growth, the index as a product, the Market Sheet).
If the founder's ambition is a company that "stands toe to toe with the largest in the
world," this plan does not have a slow path to it. It has no path to it.

## 7. Price sensitivity

| Price | SLOW ceiling ARR | BASE ceiling ARR | FAST ceiling ARR | Locations to cover all cash costs |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $400/loc/mo | $17,333 | $94,871 | $246,857 | 2.99 |
| $600/loc/mo | $26,000 | $142,306 | $370,286 | 2.0 |
| $1000/loc/mo | $43,333 | $237,176 | $617,143 | 1.2 |

**Price is the least sensitive lever in the model.** Moving $400 -> $1,000 (2.5x) moves
the BASE ceiling from $94,871 to $237,176. Moving `desk_minutes_per_close` from 45 to 12
moves it from $26,000 to $370,286 (**14x**). The company has spent fifteen months and
five posted prices arguing about the lever that matters least.

### Is per-location the right unit?

No, and the repos say so. `about/cost` publishes variable cost at **~$0.004/invoice**
while the real marginal cost is **founder-and-agent hours per distinct VENDOR LAYOUT**
(`docs/plans/first-try-reliability-roadmap.md` s3: each template needs a real reference
invoice A *and* a held-out invoice B run through production docling). The buyer is
*defined* by vendor mix — 8-12 specialty vendors, zero or one broadliner — so the cost
driver is named in the buyer definition and then not charged for.

Consequences the per-location unit produces:

- A 1-location restaurant with 14 vendors costs more to serve than a 3-location group
  with 9 shared vendors, and pays a third as much.
- The tail vendors are the expensive ones and have no market prior to fall back on:
  reconcile rates of **cintas 54.3%, staples 37.5%, cozzini_bros 20.8%** against
  i_halper 93.2% (`bones/dream-price-inversion.md`). Those are non-food vendors the
  free Cost Index covers zero of.
- DMV clustering is the correct instinct and the pricing does not reward it. A
  same-cluster customer whose layouts are already covered should be cheaper, and a
  fourteenth novel layout should cost something.

A defensible unit: **a base close fee per location plus a one-time per-novel-layout
onboarding fee, waived when the layout is already covered.** That charges the actual
cost driver, makes the DMV cluster commercially visible, and prices the tail honestly.

## 8. What this model asserts, and what it cannot

It asserts: the cost base is small and knowable; the founder-hour engine is the entire
business; the verdict's ceiling is overstated on two independent grounds (the $288K
arithmetic and the four omitted hour-loads); and the cash calendar it wrote is worse
than the one it killed.

It cannot assert a conversion rate. **Zero customers ever means zero recorded objections**
(`bones/operator-the-money.md`, Unknowns). Every acquisition number here is capacity-
driven — what the founder's hours permit — not demand-driven. If demand is the binding
constraint rather than hours, every case above is optimistic, and nothing in either repo
can tell you which it is.

## 9. Four money items with a price, sitting outside the model

### 9a. The annual prepay is the least likely assumption in the plan, and the cash model rests on it

A $6,000 check, upfront, from a single-location independent. Using the storefront's own
registered bands — food 28-35% of sales, prime 55-65%, fixed 12-14 points — net margin
lands in low single digits. At 3% net on $1M of sales the whole year's profit is ~$30,000.
**The annual invoice asks for 20% of a year's profit, in one payment, in advance, from a
one-person vendor with zero customers and no track record.**

Nothing in either repo tests this. If it fails and the deal falls back to monthly:

- the May-August seasonality routing the verdict designed **disappears entirely**, and
  charges land in January and February like everyone else's;
- hand-invoicing goes from ~20 invoices/year to **240** at 20 customers. At 10 minutes to
  issue, reconcile and chase, that is **~3.3 founder-hours/month — 17-25% of the entire
  discretionary budget**, spent on clerical work, growing linearly with customers;
- January churn exposure returns, and there is no dunning, no card on file and no
  auto-renew, because the verdict removed billing code from the critical path.

"No billing code for the first cohort" saves perhaps a day of build and costs roughly
40 founder-hours a year, forever, the moment annual prepay does not hold. It is a
reasonable call for 10 annual customers and an expensive one for 20 monthly ones. The
model should be re-run the first time a prospect says "can we do monthly."

### 9b. The Terms of Service currently forbid the product being sold

`apps/web/lib/legal-content.generated.ts:1233-1252` — the live Terms, s6:

> Muntin Ledger is a record-keeping tool. It is **not** accounting, tax, bookkeeping,
> audit, or legal advice... you are responsible for verifying every figure before you
> rely on it, pay against it, or file it.
> To the maximum extent allowed by law, Muntin's liability for any claim arising under
> these Terms is limited to the amount you have paid us in the 12 months immediately
> preceding the claim.

The strategy sells "a dated, **signed** statement of food cost... the way an audit firm
sells an opinion." Those cannot both stand. Either the disclaimer stays — in which case
the customer is told, in the contract, not to rely on the thing the $600 is charging for —
or it comes out, and the risk class changes.

Two priced consequences:

1. **The liability cap scales with the price.** At $228/yr it was $228. At $6,000/yr it
   is **$6,000 per customer** — a 26x increase in the contractual exposure, arriving as a
   side effect of a pricing decision nobody connected to the cap.
2. **E&O must be bound before the first close ships, not after.** `docs/legal-counsel-kickoff.md`
   records that the Terms of Service were never drafted by counsel ("not yet drafted; this
   engagement should produce the first draft") — the live Terms are generated from repo
   markdown by `scripts/build-legal-content.mjs`. A carrier underwriting a signed monthly
   financial statement produced by an engine the founder has never run end to end may rate
   it outside the standard tech-E&O form, or decline it. **I could not verify what that
   costs.** The $1,200-$4,800 band above is the honest range and its top end is my guess.

### 9c. Six product shells, unfunded and unretired

`apps/api`, `apps/web`, `apps/ios`, `apps/mobile`, `apps/desktop`, `apps/email-worker`.
`apps/ios/RELEASE.md` is a complete App Store Connect release runbook; `apps/mobile` ships
an Android target. Zero customers use any of them.

The strategy retires ~460 storefront pages, the audio pipeline, the bookkeeper channel, the
Reference Plate, the attestation product, four waitlist forms and five posted prices — and
says **nothing** about the product repo's own surface count. Each shell carries store fees
($99/yr Apple, $25 Google Play), annual compliance cycles, and a toolchain (Tauri, Capacitor,
Swift) that decays. The one-in-one-out retirement gate the verdict imposes on the storefront
should apply here, where the cost is a recurring bill rather than a page nobody reads.

### 9d. The number the model is a bet on is not scheduled to be measured

`grep -rn "desk_minutes"` returns **zero hits in either repo**. It appears only in
`bones/dream-price-inversion.md` and the verdict, both of which say it is the number that
decides everything.

Section 5 shows the residual ranging from **$224/year to $280,000/year** on that one
parameter. The verdict says it should be "instrumented from customer one and published" —
which sets the price before the number is known. It costs nothing to measure it earlier:
the Phase-0 twenty-invoice founder walk is already scheduled for week one, and adding a
stopwatch to it converts the single largest uncertainty in the business from a bet into a
measurement, **before** the price is posted rather than after ten customers have signed at it.
