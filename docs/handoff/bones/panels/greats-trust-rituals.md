# Trust Rituals and Radical Transparency

## Thesis
A trust ritual compounds when the artifact is a by-product of work that has to happen anyway, and rots when it is a separate act on a calendar. Berkshire's letter rides a legally required annual report; Wikipedia's edit history IS the encyclopedia's storage format; Muntin's warrant canary and quarterly transparency report exist only because someone once promised them — which is why every calendar-anchored ritual it owns is 37–89 days in arrears (canary 89d, transparency 37d, changelog 40d, /receipts/ "reviewed weekly" 41d) while every machine-derived one — the 10,294-entry revisions ledger, the 102-ingredient anomaly log, claims.json — has never once lapsed. That is the entire diagnosis: the trust artifacts are not failing because the founder is undisciplined, they are failing because he built the wrong half by hand. Worse, the company already invented the correct pattern and then implemented it manually: `runbooks/promises-last-verified.md` specifies a page where each promise's date is stamped by the CI gate that proves it, so that "a regression in a privacy guarantee produces a visibly-stale page, not a quietly-broken contract" — and then made `LAST_VERIFIED` a hand-edited constant. Wire that to the pipeline and you have a warrant canary with the ambiguity removed: decay is produced by the machine, so silence is evidence rather than a question about whether the engineer was on vacation. On being small: Buffer's credibility came from precommitting to publish the number that was most embarrassing, not from the number's size — and Muntin's equivalent is not "zero users," it is the archive's own unflattering interior (26 frozen series, 97.6% reconstructed backfill, 10,294 revisions to previously-published prices, 37 of 100 ingredients where it refuses a posture). Every one of those is already true, already in the data, says nothing about headcount, and would never be published by a rival selling a "proprietary price database" — the embarrassment is the moat. And the 72 falsehoods are not a scandal, they are the best story the company has: all 72 were true on publication day, they broke from a deliberate methodology upgrade, and Muntin found them itself. Post-mortems become trust assets when the company says it before it has to and ships the preventing mechanism in the same breath.

## Mechanisms
### Berkshire Hathaway (annual shareholder letter)
**What:** One long letter, same author, same cadence, published free alongside a document that legally must be produced anyway; it leads with mistakes named with the year and the cost, and a public archive of prior letters is kept online so any reader can score the author against his own past words. (Specific dates/dollar figures of individual mea culpas: UNVERIFIED — do not cite numbers.)

**Why it worked:** Two structural things, neither of them voice. First, the cadence is parasitic on an event that cannot be skipped, so the ritual never depends on discretionary time. Second, the persistent archive turns each new letter into an audit of every previous one — the author's incentive to be conservative today is enforced by his own back-catalogue, which is a compounding constraint no press release has.

**For Muntin:** Directly, and it is the highest-value unbuilt thing in the dispatch. The monthly Cost Index edition is Muntin's letter, and `data/cost-index-editions.json` is already the archive — an append-only frozen snapshot per asOf. What is missing is the self-scoring half: every edition should OPEN by grading the previous edition's read against what the data actually did, because the spine already holds both sides of that comparison. That converts a monthly report nobody habitually checks into a running scorecard the company keeps on itself, and it is the only feature that makes the archive earn its keep for a reader rather than for a citation.

### Cloudflare / Stripe (public incident post-mortems)
**What:** The post-mortem ships under the same masthead and with the same production values as a product announcement: a timeline, the mechanism named rather than the person, an honest statement of blast radius, and a closing section listing the specific change shipped so the class of failure cannot recur.

**Why it worked:** The customer already experienced the incident, so concealment was never available — the only variable is whether the company narrates it first. The load-bearing part is the last section: 'what we changed' is checkable, which is what separates a post-mortem from an apology. It converts an event that costs trust into the only credible demonstration that an engineering culture exists.

**For Muntin:** This is the template for the 72 published falsehoods, and Muntin's case is far more favorable than a real outage. Every one of the 72 was true at publication (the audit recovered commit `9f11e0885` and matched the prose exactly), and they broke from commit `bae8c9d31`, a deliberate 5-year-seasonality methodology upgrade. That is an error of TENSE, not of fact — a dated read written in the timeless present. Publish it as a dated Revision Notice with four fields (what was published / what is true now / why it changed, naming the commit / what mechanism now prevents it), and ship `check-src-sentinel.mjs` in the same PR so the fourth field is verifiable.

### Wikipedia (edit history + 'citation needed')
**What:** The revision history is not a report about the encyclopedia, it is the encyclopedia's storage format — every version is retained because that is how editing works. Separately, an unsourced claim carries a visible absence marker rather than being silently deleted or silently kept.

**Why it worked:** Zero marginal cost: the audit trail is a by-product of the primary activity, so it cannot lapse without the primary activity stopping. And the visible-absence marker inverts the usual incentive — incompleteness becomes an invitation rather than a flaw to conceal, which is why readers trust a page that admits its own gaps more than a page that doesn't have any.

**For Muntin:** Muntin already owns both halves and presents neither to a reader as a trust artifact. `data/cost-revisions.json` is a 10,294-entry append-only ledger of every change to a previously-published reading, built deterministically by `build-cost-revisions.mjs` and kept in sync by CI `--check` — that is Wikipedia's edit history for prices, and it is invisible. The withhold logic (refusing a posture on 37 of 100 ingredients, `seasonalUseful:false`) is 'citation needed' compiled into an exit code, also invisible. Promote both: a 'revised N times' chip on every ingredient page linking into the ledger, and a standing 'What we refused to publish, and why' page. Both are Tier-A generated artifacts that regenerate on deploy and can never lapse.

### Muntin's own `/promises` last-verified design (runbooks/promises-last-verified.md)
**What:** Each public promise is paired with the specific CI gate that proves it (`no-llm` → `scripts/no-llm-ci.sh`, `anonymous-demo` → `check-demo-no-persistence.mjs`, …) and the page renders the date that gate last ran clean. The runbook's own §1 states the design intent: 'When a gate fails, the timestamp stops updating — the page truthfully shows the stale date. This means a regression in a privacy guarantee produces a visibly-stale page, not a quietly-broken contract.'

**Why it worked:** It is the warrant-canary mechanic with the failure mode inverted. A classic canary lapses because a human forgot, so silence is ambiguous — the standard critique is that a lapse could mean an NSL or could mean vacation. Here decay is produced by the machine and is caused only by the thing it warns about, so silence is evidence. It is the only trust artifact design that is safe to promise in perpetuity for a one-person company.

**For Muntin:** It is already specified and already wrong in the one way that matters: §3 marks it 'P2 (current state — manual)' and the page reads a hand-edited `LAST_VERIFIED` constant in `apps/web/app/(marketing)/promises/page.tsx`. That converts the company's unlapseable design into another chore, and the audit found /receipts/ pointing readers at `ledger.muntin.digital/promises`, a hostname neither repo routes. Wire the stamp to the CI run, resolve the hostname, and then make this the mandatory template for every remaining trust surface.

### GitLab (public handbook)
**What:** The handbook is the PRIMARY location of company policy — not a published copy of an internal document — and it lives in a repo anyone can open a merge request against. It remains public and actively maintained (handbook.gitlab.com, commits through 2026).

**Why it worked:** The maintenance incentive is operational rather than reputational: a stale handbook means the company has no policy, so staying current is not an act of virtue but a condition of functioning. That is why it survives at a scale where a 'transparency report' would have become a chore.

**For Muntin:** Partly, and the important correction is that DISCLOSURE HAS ALREADY HAPPENED — github.com/Donwonmagic/potentially-profitable is public; `docs/` is excluded from the deploy tar, not from the world. So the question is packaging, not openness. Muntin's ADRs already have GitLab's operational property: CLAUDE.md makes them the resume-here decision record, so they cannot go stale without breaking the founder's own continuity protocol. Publish the ADR SERIES as a `/decisions/` surface generated from the markdown (26 storefront + 13 product), with status rendered. Do NOT publish the docs tree — it holds the audit, the capacity arithmetic, the Stripe bug, and seven PROPOSAL-status ADRs that would read as roadmap commitments.

### Buffer (open salaries + public revenue dashboard)
**What:** Precommitted, from near-zero, to publishing the numbers most embarrassing to publish — individual salaries with names and the formula behind them, plus a live revenue/customer dashboard — and has kept both public since 2013 (buffer.com/salaries, buffer.com/metrics still live).

**Why it worked:** The signal is the precommitment and the unflattering direction, not the magnitude. Publishing a small number you were under no obligation to publish is a costly signal that a competitor with a bigger number cannot cheaply imitate, because imitating it requires giving up the option to spin. The dashboard also removes the founder from the loop — it is fed, not written.

**For Muntin:** The salary/revenue form is unavailable: it foregrounds headcount, which the founder has explicitly ruled out, and $0 revenue with 0 users on a 'transparency dashboard' reads as a pre-launch project rather than a formidable company. But the MECHANISM transfers exactly if you point it at the data instead of the company. Build an archive-integrity dashboard: fresh vs frozen series (74 vs 26 today), live-captured vs reconstructed observations (2,025 vs 83,695), readings refused (37 of 100), revisions to previously-published prices (10,294, with the largest correction named). Every number is unflattering, every number is already in the repo, none is about headcount, and no rival selling a 'proprietary price database' would ever publish theirs.

### Basecamp / 37signals (Shape Up, published opinions)
**What:** Published the company's internal working method as a free, complete, citable book, and published sharp opinions that deliberately repel a large share of the market.

**Why it worked:** The method document is a filter, not an ad. It costs nothing to run, it qualifies inbound by worldview rather than by budget, and — critically — it creates an external ratchet: once the method is published, deviating from it is visible to outsiders, so publication is itself a commitment device.

**For Muntin:** Muntin's honesty machinery is a method with a named spec (the fact gate, `<details class="cite">`, the withhold rule, ADR-013's public-domain policy). Publishing it as a short, opinionated, citable standard — roughly 'how a price index should be allowed to say it does not know' — is a genuine category play and pure agent work. One hard caveat: the ratchet cuts both ways. The 72 falsehoods prove the method is currently enforced at the data layer and not at the prose layer. Publish the standard only AFTER `check-src-sentinel.mjs` ships, or the standard becomes the instrument of the next refutation.

### Patagonia (Footprint Chronicles)
**What:** Published its own supply chain at product granularity, including the parts that looked bad, ahead of any requirement to do so. (Current program status and any specific claims: UNVERIFIED — treat the mechanism, not the details.)

**Why it worked:** Granularity is what separated it from a sustainability report. A company-level claim is unfalsifiable; a per-product map is checkable, so publishing it converts a soft value statement into a testable assertion. It also creates an internal ratchet — once the map exists, every new product must be mappable or the gap is visible.

**For Muntin:** The per-ingredient sourcing pages already are this, and the ratchet is the part worth stealing rather than the disclosure. Make it a rule with a gate: no ingredient enters the index without a complete sourcing page, and publish the count of candidate ingredients that failed to qualify. The refusals are the credible half — a public 'we wanted to cover X and could not source it honestly' list is unfakeable and costs nothing, because the withhold logic already computes it.

## Antipatterns
- Do NOT copy Buffer's actual form — open salaries, headcount, or a revenue dashboard. It foregrounds precisely what the founder decided on 2026-07-11 not to foreground, and $0 revenue with 0 confirmed users published as a 'transparency dashboard' reads as a pre-launch side project, not a company that puts top SaaS on notice. Take Buffer's mechanism (precommit to the number that is most embarrassing to publish) and point it at the archive's interior, which is unflattering without being about size.
- Do NOT build a Netflix-style culture deck. Its mechanism is making implicit standards explicit for hiring and firing at scale; with one employee it has no analogue, and any version of it either implies a team that does not exist — crossing the founder's own hard honesty line in founder-vision.md — or admits the headcount he chose not to admit. There is no honest middle version.
- Do NOT keep the warrant canary alive by signing it late. A canary's entire value is that its lapse is legible, and the standard critique is that a lapse is ambiguous anyway — it could mean an NSL or it could mean vacation. Muntin's lapsed 89 days behind a 404 link and nobody noticed, including Muntin, which proves it had no audience and therefore all of the downside with none of the upside. Retire it publicly with a dated sentence; re-signing it just restarts a clock that has already failed three times.
- Do NOT publish the `docs/` tree as a 'handbook.' It contains this audit (zero confirmed users, the unfixed Stripe seat-multiplication bug, the 2.7x capacity deficit), the retired-line hiring fossil, and seven PROPOSAL-status ADRs that a reader would take as shipped commitments. GitLab's handbook is curated and edited; a docs dump is a liability with a table of contents. Publish the curated ADR series only, behind a gate that enforces the exclusions.
- Do NOT hand-correct the 72 numbers to today's values. It destroys the of-record property an edition exists to have, it takes hours, it will be wrong again on the next refresh, and it manufactures the identical bug for the next reader. As-of stamping converts all 72 into true historical statements while touching no number — but note the three things stamping does NOT fix and which each need their own edit: the 're-checked in CI' clause (false at any date), the FAQPage JSON-LD (the date must go INSIDE the answer string), and the two sign-inverted forward reads.
- Do NOT add any new dated public promise until the existing ones are cleared. The site currently carries five simultaneously broken ones — canary 89d, transparency 37d, changelog 40d ('corrections show in the changelog within two business days'), /receipts/ 'reviewed weekly' 41d, and 165 pages promising a first-Tuesday edition that did not arrive. Each new promise is a new decay clock on a system already ~90% in arrears on its maintenance ledger, and a sixth broken promise costs more than a first good one earns.
- Do NOT transplant Berkshire's form as 'an annual founder letter.' The letter works because it reports on capital the reader owns and is measured against a legally required filing. A founder letter at zero revenue with no shareholders is a blog post wearing a costume — and it would foreground the founder as a person, which the positioning decision rules out. Take the self-scoring mechanic into the dispatch instead, where the archive makes it real.
- Do NOT publish the honesty method as a public standard until `check-src-sentinel.mjs` ships. Publishing a method is a commitment device — it works precisely because deviation becomes visible to outsiders. Right now the method is enforced at the data layer (`count:`, `repro:` sentinels, the fact gate) and NOT at the prose layer, which is what produced 72 falsehoods on the flagship page. Publishing the standard first would hand a critic the exact instrument of the next refutation.
