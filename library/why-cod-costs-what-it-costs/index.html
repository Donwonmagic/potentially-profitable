Why cod costs
what it
costs

The United States imported $437.4 million of cod in 2025 and landed $109.3 million of it at home the year before. Four of every five dollars in the supply crosses a border, and 82% of the import value arrives from three countries. That structure is what decides how hard a cod spec moves — not a stock forecast, which this page does not make.

13 min read · By The Muntin Desk

In short

  • Imports carry about four-fifths of US cod supply by value — $464.4 million imported in 2024 against $109.3 million in domestic wild landings the same year.
  • Three origins carry 82% of that import value — China at 59%, Iceland at 12%, Vietnam at 11% — which the concentration index reads as concentrated at 0.38.
  • US cod import value has fallen three years running, from a 2022 peak of $592.5 million to $437.4 million in 2025, but customs value is nominal and mixes price with quantity, so it cannot separate less fish from cheaper fish.
  • The import stream leans on winter: January, February and December are the peak months, and the heaviest quarter carries 27% of the year’s value.
  • The Cost Index publishes no cod price because no free wholesale source clears its bars, so what follows describes measured exposure and never a forecast.

Most of the cod on a US menu crossed a border

In 2024 the United States imported $464.4 million of cod against $109.3 million in domestic wild landings. Imports run 4.25 times the domestic catch by value — about 81% of the two figures combined. Cod is an import line.

The two figures sit at different points in the chain, and the gap matters. Landings value is ex-vessel: what the boat is paid at the dock. Import value is customs value at the border, before a domestic distributor has touched it. Neither is a delivered price, and neither is what a vendor quotes on a Tuesday. The ratio between them is a supply-shape read, not a cost comparison.

What the shape says is that US fishery management sets roughly a fifth of the picture. A quota headline out of the North Atlantic or the Bering Sea moves the domestic fifth. The other four-fifths move with freight rates, currency, tariff schedules, and processing capacity in countries that do not publish their operating decisions on a schedule any operator can watch.

That is the first structural fact about cod, and it survives whatever any given stock assessment says: the price on the invoice is assembled mostly outside the jurisdiction whose fishery news makes the trade press.

Source: US Census trade data and NOAA landings, via the ingredient state record

US Census Bureau — general imports of cod under HS codes 030471, 030366 and 030251, nominal customs value, series 2012–2025. Public domain. NOAA Fisheries — US commercial landings value for cod (Atlantic and Pacific, both wild), 2024. Both figures are carried in this site's ingredient state record at the Cost Index ingredient record. Customs value is nominal and mixes price with quantity; ex-vessel value is what the vessel is paid. Neither is a wholesale or delivered price.

Three origins carry 82% of the import value

China supplies 59% of US cod import value, Iceland 12%, and Vietnam 11%. That is 82% from three origins. The concentration measure on the series reads 0.38, which the record classifies as concentrated.

Concentration is the transmission coefficient of the whole line. An interruption in an origin carrying 59% of the value moves the delivered price of nearly every cod spec in the country. The same interruption in an origin carrying 4% is absorbed by the other 96% and never reaches a walk-in. Diversity of supply is not a virtue in the abstract; it is the mechanism by which a single upstream event either does or does not arrive on an invoice.

One distinction is worth holding precisely, because it is where most cod commentary goes wrong. The Census series names the country the goods are imported from. It does not name where the fish was caught, and those are not the same question. A country can appear at the top of an import table as a processing and re-export point rather than as a fishing ground, and the trade series cannot tell the two apart. Anyone reading an import table as a map of fishing grounds is reading a document that was never about fishing grounds.

For an operator the usable form of this is narrower and more reliable: 82% of the money is exposed to conditions in three places, and the invoice does not say which of the three.

Source: US Census origin shares for the 2025 import series

US Census Bureau — 2025 source-country mix for US general imports of cod, real countries only (trade blocs and continents excluded), with a Herfindahl–Hirschman concentration index of 0.38. Public domain. Shares describe the country of import, not the country of capture. The residual "all other origins" figure is the remainder after the three named origins.

The country ships cod out and brings cod in

US domestic exports of cod ran $150.3 million in 2025 against $437.4 million of imports the same year — exports equal to 34% of the import figure. Both flows are measured, both are large, and neither cancels the other.

"Domestic exports" is a precise term in the Census series: US-produced goods leaving the country, excluding foreign re-exports. So the outbound figure is cod caught in US waters and sold abroad, not foreign product passing through. The country is a substantial exporter and a larger importer of the same fish at the same time.

The series reports flows, not routes. It records that a quantity of value left and a quantity of value arrived; it does not follow a fish from a boat through a plant to a distributor, and no public series does. Any account of what happens between those two numbers is inference, and this page does not offer one.

The operator-level consequence is small and concrete. "US-caught" and "landed in the US" are different claims about a lot, a third claim is where it was processed, and a standard invoice line answers none of the three. That is why the origin question below is worth asking out loud rather than assuming from a brand name.

The import stream leans on winter

January, February and December are the peak import months for cod, reading 1.18, 1.14 and 1.14 against a flat year of 1.00. April is the trough at 0.70. The heaviest quarter carries 27% of the year's import value.

A seasonal index is a within-year ratio: each month's share of import value measured against an even twelfth of the year, computed across the 2012–2025 series. Because it is a ratio inside each year, inflation does not distort it and a single unusual year does not set it. It describes shape, not level.

What the shape describes is when the supply crosses the border, not what it costs when it does. A January index of 1.18 means January carries about 18% more of the year's import value than an even month would. It does not mean cod is dearer in January or cheaper in April. Reading a supply-timing curve as a price curve is the same category error as reading import value as volume, and it is made about as often.

The useful read is about thinness. April is the month when the least of the year's cod import value crosses the border. A menu whose cod volume peaks in April sits at the thinnest point of the import calendar, which is a fact about timing that an operator can plan around without any view on direction.

What three years of falling import value cannot say

US cod import value peaked at $592.5 million in 2022 and has fallen every year since: $475.5 million in 2023, $464.4 million in 2024, $437.4 million in 2025. That is a 26.2% decline across three years, and 5.8% in the most recent one. It does not establish a shortage.

The reason is in the definition of the number. Customs value is nominal, and it mixes price with quantity. At the six-digit tariff level, volume is not published at all — there is no companion tonnage series to divide into the dollars. So a 26.2% fall in value is equally consistent with fewer pounds at the same price, the same pounds at a lower price, more pounds at a much lower price, or any blend of the three. The series cannot separate the variables that a scarcity claim needs separated.

The year-to-year record makes the point sharper. Import value rose 46.8% in 2022 and fell 19.7% in 2023. A 46.8% rise followed by a 19.7% fall in consecutive years is a very large swing for a series that carries a price component inside it, and any account that treats the decline since as a pure quantity signal has to explain the spike as one too.

This is the part of the cod conversation where the honest answer is a refusal. The direction of cod availability is not readable from the trade series, this publication does not forecast prices, and a number that mixes the two variables in question cannot be made to answer a question about one of them by quoting it with more confidence. What the series does support is a description of structure — which is what the rest of this page is.

US Census Bureau — annual general-import value for cod, nominal customs value, HS 030471 + 030366 + 030251. Public domain. Percentages are computed from the committed annual series: 2018 $442.3M, 2019 $425.1M, 2020 $379.9M, 2021 $403.5M, 2022 $592.5M, 2023 $475.5M, 2024 $464.4M, 2025 $437.4M. Nominal customs value is not inflation-adjusted and mixes price with quantity; import volume is not published at the six-digit tariff level, so no unit value can be derived from this series.

The public record measures two points on a long chain

Between the boat and the invoice sit a landing, a first sale, primary processing, freezing, containerised freight, customs, a domestic importer, a distributor and a delivery. The public data measures the first of those and the seventh. Everything in between is unpublished.

That is the structural reason a national cod price is hard to state honestly, and it generalises past cod. Ex-vessel value is collected because landings are reported to a fishery agency. Customs value is collected because goods crossing a border are declared. Both exist as a by-product of an administrative requirement, not because anyone set out to build a price series. No comparable requirement attaches to what a distributor charges a restaurant, so no comparable series exists.

Each unmeasured step also adds its own variance. Freezing and glazing change the ratio of paid weight to usable weight. Freight adds a cost that moves on container rates rather than on fish. Currency sits between a foreign processor's costs and a US importer's payment. A distributor's margin responds to their own volume, their own contracts, and how recently they last repriced. By the time a number reaches a receiving sheet it has passed through several markets, only two of which publish.

This is why an operator's own invoice history outperforms every public series for the decision in front of them. The invoice is the only observation taken at the point where the money actually leaves — the end of the chain, in their region, for their form, from their vendor. The public series describe the conditions upstream of that number; they do not contain it.

The Cost Index carries no cod reading

Cod is a declared ingredient in the Cost Index source registry with no published price. The Index vendors a level only when a free, public wholesale source clears its verification, provenance and plausibility bars. For cod, none does.

The rule behind that is worth stating plainly, because it is the same rule that makes the rest of the readings usable. A price ships when it is verified, sourced, inside a plausibility band, and current. When a source cannot carry a number to that standard, the Index publishes nothing rather than a proxy dressed as a measurement. A withheld number is an honest output; an estimate presented at the same weight as a measurement is not.

Cod sits in the group where no free wholesale series exists at the needed quality. Several whitefish and seafood lines sit in the same group for the same reason: the public data covers trade and landings well and covers delivered wholesale badly. That is a gap in public data, not a judgement about the ingredient.

Which is why this page describes supply structure and stops there. The price signal an operator actually has for cod is their own invoice history — a measured series, specific to their vendor, their form, and their region, and better than any national average for the only decision they have to make.

Exposure is a measurement; a forecast is not

Three measured properties set how hard a cod line moves: import share at about 81%, origin concentration at 0.38, and a peak quarter carrying 27% of annual value. None of the three predicts a price. Together they describe amplitude.

Exposure is the share of a line's cost that is set outside the operator's control and outside a single jurisdiction. On that definition cod scores high on every available axis. Four-fifths of the value crosses a border, so domestic policy governs a minority of it. Four-fifths of the imported share arrives from three countries, so the number of independent things that can move it is small. The stream is seasonally uneven, so the calendar adds its own variance on top.

A line with those three properties moves further, in whichever direction it moves, than a line that is domestically supplied from many sources at an even rate through the year. That is a statement about amplitude and not about direction, and it is the strongest claim the public data supports.

It is also the more useful claim. A forecast, if one existed and were right, would tell an operator what to do once. An exposure reading tells them how much slack to build into a spec permanently — which is the decision that recurs.

The moves that work in either direction

A spec that survives a price move up also survives one down. Four moves need no view on which way cod goes: spec by yield, name the swap in advance, set a trigger, and date the quote.

Spec by yield, not by invoice price. The cost that reaches a plate is the invoice price divided by the usable weight — the yield percent — and form drives the second term. Fresh, once-frozen and twice-frozen fillets of the same nominal weight do not deliver the same portion count, and a lot that arrives glazed carries water an operator paid for by the pound. A spec written against portion cost absorbs a form change; a spec written against invoice price discovers it at the pass. The Plate Cost Calculator holds the arithmetic if the spec sheet does not.

Name the swap while nothing is happening. Haddock and pollock are declared in the same source registry as cod and occupy the same slot on most menus that carry a white fish. The time to decide which one replaces cod, at what portion, at what menu price, is a quiet Tuesday — not the morning a delivery lands short. A named swap is a decision already made; an unnamed one is a decision made at the walk-in by whoever is standing there.

Set a trigger price, not a reprint schedule. Menus get reprinted on a calendar and prices move on their own clock, so a calendar reprint is always either early or late. A trigger — a number on the spec sheet that prompts a review when the invoice crosses it — ties the response to the thing that actually changed. It also converts a vague unease about cod into a specific figure someone can check.

Date the quote and record the origin. A price with no date attached cannot be compared to anything, and a lot with no recorded origin cannot be placed against the 82% concentration above. Two fields on the receiving sheet turn a stack of invoices into the price series that the public data does not provide.

The questions that get a usable answer

"Is cod going up?" returns an opinion, and the person answering has no better series than anyone else. Three questions return facts a spec can hold: the origin on this lot, the form, and the date the price was set.

Where is this lot from? Given that 82% of import value arrives from three origins, the answer places the lot in one of a small number of exposure profiles. It also tells an operator whether two quotes are comparable at all — two prices from different origins in different forms are not two data points on one series, they are one point each on two.

What form is it, exactly? Fresh, once-frozen, twice-frozen, glazed and the glaze percentage, skin on or off, pin bones in or out. Each answer moves the yield, and yield is the term that converts an invoice price into a portion cost. An invoice line that reads only "cod fillet" has left the decisive variable blank.

When was this price set? A quote carried forward from a previous cycle is a different number from one struck this week, and vendors are not uniform about which they are quoting. The date turns the answer into a dated read, which is the only kind that can be compared to the next one.

None of the three questions asks anyone to predict anything. They ask what is already known and merely unstated, which is where most of the recoverable margin on a high-exposure protein sits. The trade data describes a supply that is 81% imported, 82% concentrated in three origins, and seasonally uneven — and then stops, because that is where the public record stops. What comes after it is a spec sheet, a dated invoice, and a named swap.

Key takeaways

  • About 81% of the US cod supply picture crosses a border by value, so most of the line is set outside US fishery management.
  • Concentration is the transmission coefficient: with 82% of import value from three origins, an event in one of them reaches nearly every cod spec in the country.
  • Nominal customs value mixes price with quantity and no volume is published at the six-digit tariff level, so three years of falling import value cannot establish a shortage.
  • Exposure is measurable and direction is not — spec by yield, name the swap in advance, set a trigger price, and date the vendor quote.
